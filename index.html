01

Anatomy of a Candle

Every candle is a four-number argument between buyers and sellers, settled over one time period: open, high, low, close.

A candle isn't a shape — it's a compressed story. The body is the distance between open and close; the wicks (or shadows) are the high and low that price rejected before settling back inside the body. A long wick means price visited a level and got rejected from it hard. A tiny body with long wicks on both ends means neither side won — indecision.

Explain it like I'm 5

Think of a candle like a report card for one day of tug-of-war between Team Buyers and Team Sellers. The fat middle part shows who ended up winning and by how much. The thin lines poking out the top and bottom show how far each team got pulled before getting yanked back.

Sa Tagalog: Parang score card ng laban ng bumibili (buyer) at nagbebenta (seller) sa loob ng isang araw. Kung berde, nanalo yung bumibili. Kung pula, nanalo yung nagbenta. Yung manipis na linya sa taas at baba parang tug-of-war — may sandaling natulak sila palayo pero nabawi rin.

02

Candlestick Patterns Worth Knowing

A handful of shapes recur often enough to earn names. They mean nothing in isolation — context (trend, location, volume) is what makes them worth watching.

Explain it like I'm 5

These shapes are like facial expressions. A hammer looks like someone got knocked down and immediately bounced back up strong. A doji looks like a shrug — a tie, nobody won. The shape alone doesn't tell you the whole story, same as a face alone doesn't tell you what someone's really thinking.

Sa Tagalog: Parang facial expression ng market ang mga pattern na ito. Yung "hammer" parang natumba pero bumangon agad nang malakas. Yung "doji" parang balikat lang — walang nanalo, parang tabla (tie). Hindi porket may expression, alam mo na agad ang buong kwento.

Doji indecision

Open ≈ close, long wicks either side. Neither side controlled the period. Common at turning points, useless as a signal on its own.

Hammer reversal

Small body near the top, long lower wick, little/no upper wick. After a downtrend, shows sellers pushed price down and buyers slammed it back up.

Shooting Star reversal

Mirror of the hammer — small body near the bottom, long upper wick. After an uptrend, shows a failed push higher.

Bullish Engulfing reversal

A red candle fully swallowed by the next green candle's body. Momentum flipped hands within one period.

Bearish Engulfing reversal

Same idea, inverted — a green candle engulfed by the next red one. Often marks exhaustion at a high.

Inside Bar compression

A candle whose entire range sits inside the prior candle's range. Volatility is coiling — often precedes an expansion move.

03

Trend & Market Structure

Trend isn't a slope you eyeball — it's a sequence of highs and lows you can point to.

An uptrend is a series of higher highs (HH) and higher lows (HL): every pullback finds support above the last one. A downtrend is the mirror — lower highs (LH) and lower lows (LL). The market is only "trending" while that sequence keeps printing. The moment a higher-low fails to hold, or a lower-high gets broken, structure has shifted.

Explain it like I'm 5

An uptrend is like climbing a staircase — sometimes you step back down one stair, but you never go lower than the last time you stepped back, so overall you keep going up. A downtrend is walking down that same staircase — even a step up doesn't get you higher than before.

Sa Tagalog: Ang uptrend parang umaakyat ka ng hagdan — minsan bumababa ka ng isang baitang, pero hindi ka bumababa nang mas mababa sa huling pagbaba mo, kaya pataas pa rin overall. Ang downtrend, kabaliktaran — kahit tumaas ka ng konti, hindi mo pa rin naaabot yung dating pinakamataas.

BOS continuation

Break of Structure — price takes out the prior swing high (in an uptrend) confirming the trend is continuing.

CHoCH warning

Change of Character — the first higher-low or lower-high fails. Doesn't confirm reversal, but tells you to stop assuming continuation.

04

Support & Resistance

Think in zones, not lines. Price rarely respects a level to the exact tick.

Support is a price area where buying pressure has repeatedly overwhelmed selling; resistance is the reverse. The more times a zone has been tested, the more traders are watching it — which is exactly why zones eventually break: everyone's stop or order is sitting in the same place. When a resistance zone finally breaks, it frequently flips and becomes support on the retest — role reversal, one of the most reliable structural ideas in price action.

Explain it like I'm 5

Support is like a floor a ball keeps bouncing on. Resistance is like a ceiling the ball keeps bumping its head on. Here's the fun part: if the ball finally punches through the floor and falls to a new level, that same floor can turn into the ceiling if the ball tries to climb back up — like a trapdoor that only opens one way.

Sa Tagalog: Ang support parang sahig na tinatalbugan ng bola. Ang resistance parang kisame na binabangga ng bola paakyat. Kapag nabasag yung sahig at bumagsak yung bola sa mas mababa, pwedeng maging bagong kisame yung dating sahig kapag sumubok itong bumalik pataas — parang bitag na iisang direksyon lang ang bukas.
Field noteDraw zones with the wicks, not just the closes — the wick is where the market actually rejected. A zone drawn only from closing prices is often too narrow to mean anything on a retest.
05

Liquidity & Stop Hunts

Every obvious high or low has orders parked just beyond it — and price knows exactly where they're sitting.

When two swing highs land at roughly the same price, everyone can see it — which is exactly the problem. Traders who sold at that level have stop-losses resting just above it. Breakout traders have buy-stop orders resting just above it too, ready to trigger the moment price finally clears the level. That whole cluster of resting orders is called liquidity. Large players often need a lot of opposing orders to fill their own size without moving price too far against themselves — and a cluster of stops is exactly that. It's common for price to wick briefly through an obvious high or low, trigger that resting liquidity, and then reverse hard back the other way.

Explain it like I'm 5

Imagine a fisherman who knows exactly where all the fish like to gather, so he casts his net right there instead of randomly. Big traders do something similar: they know where everyone's "safety exit" orders are parked — just above an obvious high, just below an obvious low — so price gets pushed there on purpose to scoop those orders up before the real move happens.

Sa Tagalog: Isipin mo mangingisda na alam na alam kung saan nagtitipon ang mga isda, kaya doon niya inihahagis ang lambat imbes na kung saan-saan. Ganito rin ang ginagawa ng malalaking traders — alam nila kung saan nakatambak ang mga "safety exit" order ng mga tao, kaya sinasadyang idaan doon ang presyo para masagap muna iyon bago ang tunay na galaw.
Field noteThis is the same mechanic you've already met twice under different names — the Wyckoff Spring in Section 09 and the role-reversal retest in Section 04. "Liquidity grab" is just the name for what's actually happening underneath both: a brief move beyond an obvious level to collect the orders resting there.

Chart Patterns

Zoom out from a single candle to a whole shape, and price tends to repeat a handful of them — because the crowd behind the tape repeats the same behaviors.

These fall into three families. Reversal patterns (double top, head & shoulders, and their inverses) mark a trend running out of energy and turning the other way. Continuation patterns (flags, pennants, rectangles) are pauses — the market catching its breath mid-trend before carrying on in the same direction. Bilateral patterns (triangles) can break either way — the shape itself doesn't tell you which, only that volatility is compressing before a decision.

Explain it like I'm 5

A double top looks like the letter M and a double bottom looks like a W — price tried twice to break through and failed, twice. A head and shoulders looks exactly like it sounds: a bump, a bigger bump, a bump — three tries, with the middle one the strongest, before it gives up. A flag or triangle is the market pausing to catch its breath mid-run, like a runner slowing to a jog before sprinting again.

Sa Tagalog: Ang double top parang letrang M at ang double bottom parang W — dalawang beses sinubukan ng presyo tumawid pero nabigo nang dalawang beses. Ang head and shoulders literal na parang ulo at balikat — tatlong subok, yung gitna ang pinakamalakas, bago sumuko. Ang flag o triangle parang tumitigil lang sandali para huminga ang market habang tumatakbo, bago tumakbo ulit.
Field noteTreat the entry/stop/target lines drawn on both sheets as illustrations of where those three things tend to sit relative to the pattern — not fixed rules to copy onto your own chart. Your actual stop still comes from Section 04's zones (beyond the pattern's own high or low), and your position size still comes from the mentor letter's 1% math. A pattern tells you a shape formed; it doesn't set your risk for you.
07

Reading Volume

Price tells you what happened. Volume tells you how much conviction was behind it.

A breakout on heavy volume means real participation pushed price through a level — it's more likely to hold. A breakout on thin volume is easier to fade; there may not be enough behind it to sustain the move. Watch for two recurring tells: climax volume (an extreme volume spike after an extended move, often marking exhaustion rather than continuation) and declining volume on a pullback (a healthy sign in a trend — the retracement lacks conviction, so the prior move is likely to resume).

Explain it like I'm 5

Volume is like how loud the crowd cheers. A big move with a huge, loud cheer means tons of people really showed up and believe in it. A big move with barely any clapping means almost nobody showed up — it might not last.

Sa Tagalog: Ang volume parang lakas ng sigawan ng crowd. Kung malaking galaw ng presyo na may kasamang malakas na sigawan, ibig sabihin marami talaga sumali at naniniwala. Kung tahimik lang habang malaki yung galaw, baka konti lang sumali at hindi tatagal.

CME Gaps — Specific to Bitcoin Futures

This one matters directly for the chart you screenshotted: CME:BTC1! trades on exchange hours. Spot Bitcoin never sleeps.

CME Bitcoin futures only trade roughly Sunday 5pm CT through Friday 4pm CT, with a short daily maintenance break. Spot Bitcoin trades 24/7/365 on crypto exchanges. If spot price moves meaningfully over a weekend while CME is closed, the futures chart reopens Sunday evening at wherever spot now sits — leaving a visible empty space on the candlestick chart between Friday's close and Sunday's open. That empty space is the CME gap.

Explain it like I'm 5

Imagine a shop (CME) that closes on weekends, but the shop right next door (spot Bitcoin) never closes, ever. If a big sale happens next door over the weekend and the price changes a lot, when the first shop reopens Monday it has to suddenly jump to match — leaving a gap in its own price chart from where it left off Friday.

Sa Tagalog: Isipin mo tindahan (CME) na sarado tuwing weekend, pero yung katabi (spot Bitcoin) bukas talaga 24/7, walang tulog. Kung nagbago ang presyo doon habang sarado yung una, pagbukas nito ulit sa Lunes kailangan nitong tumalon papunta sa bagong presyo — kaya may puwang o "gap" sa chart nila mula noong huling sarado sila.
Field noteGap-fill is a tendency traders lean on, not a law of physics. Treat an open gap as a magnet worth marking on your chart — never as a trade signal by itself. Combine it with structure and volume before acting.

Spotting Accumulation

Big buyers can't fill a position in one candle without moving price against themselves — so they build it quietly, inside a range. Wyckoff's schematic is the classic map of what that looks like.

After a hard decline, "smart money" accumulation often follows a recognizable rhythm: a climactic sell-off, a snap-back rally, a sideways range where supply is absorbed, one final shakeout below the range to trigger stops and grab liquidity, and then a break upward on expanding volume.

Explain it like I'm 5

Imagine a whale wants to buy every mango at the market without the vendor noticing and raising the price. So the whale buys a little at a time, quietly, for weeks — the price just sits still. Right before leaving, the whale even pretends to walk away (scaring other buyers into selling cheap) so it can scoop up the last cheap mangoes — then finally lets everyone see it wants more, and the price shoots up.

Sa Tagalog: Isipin mo may "malaking pating" (big investor) na gustong bilhin lahat ng mangga sa palengke nang hindi napapansin ng tindera, para hindi tumaas agad ang presyo. Kaya dahan-dahan siyang bumibili nang tahimik habang nakatengga lang ang presyo. Bago umalis, nagkukunwari pa siyang aalis (para matakot yung iba at magbenta ng mura) — doon niya sinasagap yung natitira. Saka lang niya babatuk-batukin tumaas nang husto ang presyo.

Moving Averages & Bollinger Bands

Two overlays worth turning on from that indicator search dialog you screenshotted. Both are just math on top of price — the skill is knowing what the math is telling you.

A moving average (MA) is the average closing price over the last N candles, redrawn every candle so it "moves." It smooths out noise and shows you the trend without the zig-zag. MA20 (roughly one trading month on a daily chart) tracks the short-term trend and reacts fast. MA200 (roughly nine to ten months) is the slow one institutions watch as a line in the sand for the broader regime — price spending most of its time above MA200 is a structurally bullish market; below it, structurally bearish.

Field noteThe famous "Golden Cross" / "Death Cross" is usually built from the 50-day and 200-day MAs, not the 20 and 200 — a slower, less noisy pair. A 20/200 cross fires far more often and far earlier, which makes it faster but also far more prone to false signals. Know which pair you're looking at before you trust a cross.
Explain it like I'm 5

A moving average is like your average grade over your last 20 or 200 quizzes. One bad quiz doesn't crash it and one lucky quiz doesn't spike it — it moves slowly, so it shows you the real overall direction: are you actually improving, or actually slipping?

Sa Tagalog: Ang moving average parang average grades mo sa huling 20 o 200 quiz. Hindi basta babagsak o tataas dahil lang sa isang quiz — dahan-dahan ang galaw niya, kaya makikita mo yung totoong direksyon mo: tumataas ba talaga o bumababa ang grades mo overall.

Both MAs also work as dynamic support and resistance: in a healthy uptrend, pullbacks often stall right at the MA20 and bounce. The further price stretches away from an MA, the more "extended" it is — and the more likely a mean-reversion snap back toward it.

Bollinger Bands take this further: a middle band (usually the MA20), plus an upper and lower band drawn two standard deviations away from it. Since standard deviation measures how spread out price has been, the bands aren't fixed — they widen when volatility expands and squeeze tight when volatility dries up. A tight squeeze after a long quiet stretch is one of the more reliable warnings that a big directional move is loading, in either direction.

Explain it like I'm 5

Bollinger Bands are like a rubber band wrapped around a bouncy ball. When the ball is calm and barely moving, the rubber band sits tight and narrow. When the ball suddenly bounces wild and hard, the rubber band stretches wide open. A very tight, narrow rubber band after a calm stretch usually means a big bounce is coming soon.

Sa Tagalog: Ang Bollinger Bands parang rubber band sa paligid ng tumatalbog na bola. Kapag tahimik lang ang bola, masikip at makitid yung rubber band. Kapag biglang lumundag nang malakas, lumalawak yung rubber band. Kapag matagal nang masikip yung rubber band pagkatapos ng katahimikan, malapit na yatang lumundag nang malakas ang bola.

In a range mean-reversion

Price tagging the outer band with no trend behind it often snaps back toward the middle band (the MA20).

In a trend continuation

Price can "walk" the upper (or lower) band for many candles in a row. Treat repeated band touches as trend strength, not an automatic reversal signal.

Fibonacci Retracement & Extension

A pullback almost never retraces 100% of a move — and certain partial levels keep getting tested, because enough traders are watching the same ones.

Draw a line from a swing low to a swing high (in an uptrend) and a charting platform will divide that leg into ratios pulled from the Fibonacci sequence: 23.6%, 38.2%, 50%, 61.8%, and 78.6%. These become horizontal levels where a pullback often stalls before the trend resumes — the 61.8% zone in particular is nicknamed the golden pocket. Beyond the original high, extension levels like 127.2% and 161.8% mark where the next leg might reasonably run out of steam — useful as a rough profit-target zone, not an exact number.

Explain it like I'm 5

Stretch a rubber band from a table up to your hand, then let a little tension out. It doesn't snap all the way back down to the table — it settles at a spot partway down. Fibonacci levels are the market's favorite "partway down" spots, and they work partly because so many traders are watching those exact same spots at the same time.

Sa Tagalog: Iunat mo yung rubber band mula sa mesa papunta sa kamay mo, tapos bitawan mo ng konti. Hindi ito babalik nang buo sa mesa — may parte kung saan ito huminto sa gitna. Ganyan din ang mga Fibonacci level — paboritong "paghinto sa gitna" ng market, at gumagana ito dahil marami ring traders ang nakatutok sa parehong mga parteng iyon.
Field noteFib levels are among the most self-fulfilling tools in this notebook — useful precisely because so many people draw the same lines, not because of any hidden law of markets. Treat a fib zone as a place to look for confirmation (a candle pattern, a volume shift, a structure retest) rather than a signal on its own.

Momentum: RSI & MACD

Price tells you where the market went. Momentum tells you how hard it's still trying to get there.

RSI (Relative Strength Index) compares the size of recent up-candles to recent down-candles and scales the result from 0 to 100. Above 70 is usually called overbought, below 30 oversold — but in a strong trend RSI can sit pinned near an extreme for a long stretch, so treat those zones as "worth watching," not an automatic signal. MACD plots the gap between a fast and a slow moving average (12 and 26 periods) against its own 9-period average — when the two cross, or when the whole thing crosses zero, momentum is shifting direction.

The single most useful thing either tool does is spot divergence: price making a new low while the oscillator makes a higher low. Price is still falling, but the force behind the fall is already weakening — often the earliest warning that a downtrend is running out of sellers (and the mirror version, at highs, warns a rally is running out of buyers).

Explain it like I'm 5

RSI is like a speedometer strapped to a runner — it tells you how tired and fast they're going, not how far they've run. Divergence is spotting a runner who's still moving forward but is clearly out of breath, wobbling, slowing down — a good sign they're about to stop, even though they haven't stopped yet.

Sa Tagalog: Ang RSI parang speedometer na nakakabit sa isang tumatakbo — sinasabi nito kung gaano siya kapagod at kabilis, hindi kung gaano na siya kalayo natakbo. Ang divergence ay ang pagkita sa isang tumatakbo pa rin pasulong pero halatang naubusan na ng hininga — senyales na malapit na siyang tumigil, kahit hindi pa siya tumitigil.
Field noteBoth indicators lag price — they're built from it, not ahead of it. "Overbought" in a strong uptrend can stay overbought for weeks. Use RSI and MACD to confirm what structure and volume are already telling you, not to fight an obvious trend on your own.

Timeframes & Top-Down Analysis

The same candle can be an entire trend on one timeframe and a single blip on another — always know which map you're holding.

Work top-down, not bottom-up. Start on a higher timeframe (Daily or Weekly) to read the dominant trend and mark the major support/resistance zones — this is your bias. Drop to a middle timeframe (4H or 1H) to see the current structure and whatever pattern is forming inside that bias. Only then drop to a lower timeframe (15m or 5m) to fine-tune exactly where you'd enter. The lower timeframe's job is timing, never overruling the direction the higher timeframe already set.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like using Google Maps. You zoom out to see the whole country first so you know which direction you're generally headed. Then you zoom into the city, then the street, to find the exact door. Nobody uses a world map to parallel park — and nobody should use a 15-minute chart to decide whether the overall trend is up or down.

Sa Tagalog: Parang Google Maps — muna mong titingnan yung buong bansa (Daily) para malaman kung saang direksyon ka pupunta. Tapos zoom in ka sa siyudad (4H), tapos sa kalye (15-minuto) para mahanap yung eksaktong pinto. Walang gumagamit ng mapa ng buong mundo para lang mag-park ng sasakyan — at hindi dapat ginagamit ang 15-minutong chart para malaman kung pataas ba o pababa talaga ang market overall.
Field noteIf your 15-minute chart and your Daily chart disagree, the Daily wins the argument. A perfect-looking lower-timeframe setup against the higher-timeframe trend is a lower-probability trade, not a free pass to ignore the bigger picture.

Order Types & Futures Mechanics

Two different things worth knowing cold: the buttons you'll actually click, and what makes a continuous futures symbol like BTC1! behave the way it does.

Market Order

"Fill me right now, at whatever the current price is." Fastest way in or out — but on thin liquidity, your actual fill can slip worse than the price you saw.

Limit Order

"Only fill me at this price or better." You control the price, not the timing — it might never fill if the market never comes back to it.

Stop Order

"If price reaches this level, become a market order." The standard way to place a stop-loss or a breakout entry — can still slip in a fast move.

Stop-Limit

"If price reaches this level, place a limit order at this price." Protects against slippage, but in a fast move it can fail to fill at all.

Now the part specific to what you're actually charting: BTC1! isn't one single contract — it's a continuous contract, a stitched-together feed that always shows whichever quarterly CME Bitcoin futures contract is currently the front month, then automatically switches to the next one as expiration approaches. The real, tradable contracts underneath it expire quarterly (March, June, September, December) and are cash-settled — there's no actual bitcoin delivered.

Explain it like I'm 5

A market order is like buying a ticket from a scalper at whatever price they're asking, right now, because you need in immediately. A limit order is like haggling — you leave your offer and walk away, and they only call you if they accept it. A stop order is like a fire alarm wired to a sprinkler: the moment the trigger level is hit, it goes off automatically, no matter what else is happening. And BTC1! is like a relay race — it's not one runner the whole time, it's whoever is currently holding the baton, handed off every few months.

Sa Tagalog: Ang market order parang bibili ka ng ticket sa scalper kahit magkano na, dahil kailangan mo na talaga agad. Ang limit order parang pagtawad — iiwan mo lang yung alok mo at aalis, tatawagan ka lang nila kung tinanggap nila. Ang stop order parang alarm ng sunog na naka-kabit sa sprinkler — sa sandaling maabot ang trigger level, kusa itong gagalaw kahit ano pa ang nangyayari. At ang BTC1! parang relay race — hindi iisang tumatakbo mula umpisa hanggang wakas, kundi sinumang may hawak ng baton ngayon, na nagpapalit-palit tuwing ilang buwan.
Field noteIf you ever place a real order (not just study the chart), check which specific expiration month you're actually trading — the continuous BTC1! chart is for reading history, not for clicking buttons on.

Trading Psychology

The chart doesn't move your account balance. Your reaction to the chart does.

Every rule in the "Before You Start" letter exists to fight one of a handful of predictable failure modes. Here they are by name, so you can catch yourself mid-mistake instead of only in the postmortem.

FOMO

Jumping into a move that already ran, without a plan, purely because you're afraid of missing it. Fix: if you missed the setup, you missed the trade — there is always another one.

Revenge Trading

Trying to immediately win back a loss with a bigger, less-planned trade. Fix: the "two losses ends the day" rule exists specifically for this.

Overconfidence

A win streak convincing you the rules don't apply right now, so size creeps up and the plan gets skipped. Fix: grade every trade by process, win or lose, so a streak can't rewrite your rules.

Confirmation Bias

Only noticing the chart details that agree with the trade you already wanted to take. Fix: the Section 24 checklist exists to make you check the boxes that could prove you wrong too.

Sunk-Cost Holding

Refusing to close a loser because "it'll come back," moving the stop further instead of admitting the plan failed. Fix: the stop was set by a calmer version of you, before the trade — trust that version, not the one currently staring at red numbers.

Explain it like I'm 5

Trading feelings are a rollercoaster — from "best day ever" to "worst day ever" in the space of a few candles, before you've had any practice controlling that ride. The market doesn't know or care how you feel. But if you let your feelings drive, they will absolutely hijack a perfectly good plan.

Sa Tagalog: Ang emosyon sa pagtrade parang rollercoaster — mula sa "pinakamasayang araw" hanggang "pinakamalungkot na araw" sa loob lang ng ilang candle, bago ka pa man masanay kontrolin ito. Walang pakialam ang market sa nararamdaman mo. Pero kung hahayaan mong ang emosyon ang mangunot, ito mismo ang sisira sa magandang plano mo.
Field noteNotice which of these five you personally lean toward — most traders have one or two favorites. Write it at the top of your journal (Section 26) and check for it specifically, the same way you'd check a blind spot before changing lanes.

A profitable strategy can still bust an account if the bet size is wrong. This is the math the "1% rule" in the mentor letter is actually protecting you from.

Even a strategy with a real edge will lose several trades in a row sometimes — that's normal, not a sign something's broken. What matters is whether your account can survive that losing streak. Risking a small, fixed percentage per trade means a bad stretch dents the account; risking a large percentage means the same stretch can wipe it out, even if the strategy itself was fine the whole time.

Leverage & Margin

Futures are traded on margin by default — a small deposit controls a much bigger position. That cuts both ways, always.

Leverage means a small amount of your own money (the margin) controls a much larger position. It doesn't create profit out of nothing — it multiplies whatever the underlying price move already was, gains and losses alike. Maintenance margin is the minimum equity your broker requires you to keep in the position; fall below it and you get a margin call (add funds or the position gets force-closed) or outright liquidation (the position is closed for you, often at a worse price than you'd have chosen).

Explain it like I'm 5

Leverage is a magnifying glass pointed at whatever already happened to the price — it doesn't invent extra money, it just makes the real result look and feel much bigger, good or bad. A margin call is like a loan officer calling to say your collateral isn't covering the loan anymore, and if you don't add more right now, they'll take back what's theirs.

Sa Tagalog: Ang leverage parang magnifying glass na nakatutok sa anumang nangyari na sa presyo — hindi ito gumagawa ng extra pera, pinapalaki lang nito ang totoong resulta, maganda man o masama. Ang margin call parang tumatawag na loan officer na nagsasabing hindi na sapat ang collateral mo sa utang — kung hindi ka magdadagdag agad, babawiin nila ang sa kanila.
Field noteHigher leverage means your stop-loss distance (in price) has to be tighter for the same dollar risk from the mentor letter's math — it doesn't let you skip that math, it makes getting it wrong more expensive, faster.

VWAP (Volume-Weighted Average Price)

The average price everyone actually traded at today, weighted by how much volume happened at each level — not just a plain average of the high and low.

VWAP resets at the start of each session and updates candle by candle, weighting each price by how much volume traded there. Institutions often use it as a fairness benchmark — trying to buy below VWAP or sell above it means you got a better-than-average deal for the session. As a read: price consistently above VWAP suggests buyers are in control for the day so far; consistently below suggests sellers are.

Explain it like I'm 5

VWAP is like figuring out the average price everyone paid for gas today at one station, weighted by how many people bought at each price — not just averaging the morning price and the evening price. It tells you whether the price you paid was a good deal or a bad deal compared to everyone else today.

Sa Tagalog: Ang VWAP parang paghahanap ng average presyong binayaran ng lahat ng bumili ng gas ngayong araw sa istasyong iyon, batay sa dami ng bumili sa bawat presyo — hindi lang basic average ng presyo sa umaga at hapon. Sinasabi nito kung mura o mahal ang binili mo kumpara sa lahat ng ibang bumili ngayong araw.
Field noteVWAP is an intraday tool by design — it resets every session, so it means the most on lower timeframes (5m–1H). On the Daily chart you've been practicing on, MA20 from Section 10 is doing a similar job.

Order Book & Bid-Ask Spread

Behind every candle is a live list of everyone's current offers — the order book is where that list lives.

The order book shows resting bids (buy orders) below the current price and resting asks or offers (sell orders) above it. The highest bid and the lowest ask define the spread — the small gap you effectively pay to trade immediately with a market order instead of waiting with a limit order. A "thick" book with large size stacked near the current price is liquid and harder to move; a "thin" book can let a modest order jump price several ticks.

Explain it like I'm 5

The order book is like a whiteboard at a garage sale listing everyone's current offers — buyers write what they're willing to pay, the seller writes what they're asking. The spread is the gap between the highest offer to buy and the lowest asking price to sell — the little bit of wiggle room in the middle.

Sa Tagalog: Ang order book parang blackboard sa garage sale na nakalista ang lahat ng alok — sinusulat ng mga bibili kung magkano ang kaya nilang bayaran, at sinusulat ng nagbebenta kung magkano ang hinihingi niya. Ang spread ay ang agwat sa pagitan ng pinakamataas na alok bumili at pinakamababang hinihinging presyo — ang bahagyang puwang sa gitna.
Field noteA wider-than-usual spread is itself information — it often shows up right before or during high-impact news (Section 23), when market makers pull back because they don't want to get caught quoting a stale price.

Elliott Wave Theory

A trend rarely moves in one straight push — it tends to unfold in a repeating rhythm of five waves forward, three waves back.

The theory: a trend advances in five waves (1–2–3–4–5), where waves 1, 3, and 5 move with the trend and waves 2 and 4 are corrective pullbacks against it — wave 3 is typically the longest and strongest. That five-wave move is then followed by a three-wave correction (A–B–C) against the whole thing, before the next five-wave sequence can begin. Two loose rules traders lean on: wave 2 shouldn't retrace past the very start of wave 1, and wave 4 usually shouldn't trade back into wave 1's price territory.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like watching ocean waves roll in: a big wave forward, a little pull back, another big wave forward, a smaller pull back, one more big push — then a rest of three smaller waves before the next big set arrives. Nature repeats this same rhythm at different sizes — a small trend often looks like a tiny copy of a bigger one.

Sa Tagalog: Parang panonood ng mga alon sa dagat — malaking alon pasulong, konting atras, malaking alon ulit, mas maliit na atras, isa pang malakas na tulak — tapos may pahingang tatlong maliliit na alon bago dumating ang susunod na malaking hanay. Umuulit ang kalikasan ng ritmong ito sa iba't ibang sukat — madalas ang isang maliit na trend ay parang maliit na kopya lang ng mas malaking trend.
Field noteTen different traders can count ten different waves on the same chart in real time — the count is usually only obvious after the fact. Use Elliott Wave to think about rhythm and where a move might be tiring, never as a precise entry trigger by itself.

Harmonic Patterns

A more precise cousin of the Fibonacci tool from Section 11 — patterns built from exact, measured ratios instead of a general shape.

Harmonic patterns (Gartley, Bat, Butterfly, Crab, and others) map a five-point swing labeled X-A-B-C-D, where each leg must retrace or extend the previous one by a specific Fibonacci ratio. A classic Gartley, for example, wants point B to retrace close to 61.8% of the X-A leg, and point D to complete around a 78.6% retracement of that same leg — where several of those ratios converge is called the PRZ, the Potential Reversal Zone.

Explain it like I'm 5

Regular chart patterns are like sketching a shape freehand. Harmonic patterns are like a dance with an exact, measured choreography — every step has to land within a specific percentage, not just "roughly about there."

Sa Tagalog: Ang regular na chart pattern ay parang libreng pagguhit ng hugis. Ang harmonic pattern ay parang sayaw na may eksaktong sukat sa bawat hakbang — kailangang tumapat sa partikular na porsyento ang bawat parte, hindi lang basta-basta "malapit na."
Field noteHarmonic patterns are precise enough that most traders use dedicated scanning tools rather than drawing them by eye. Treat this section as knowing the vocabulary and the idea behind it, not a requirement to spot one manually.

Ichimoku Cloud

Every tool so far describes the past or the present. This is the one indicator in the notebook that draws a projection of the future, right on the chart.

Ichimoku plots five lines from simple high/low midpoints: the fast Tenkan-sen (9-period) and slower Kijun-sen (26-period) behave like the MA crossover from Section 10 — a Tenkan cross above Kijun is a similar signal to a golden cross. A thick cloud marks a strong support/resistance zone; a thin one breaks easily.

Explain it like I'm 5

ATR (Average True Range)

Bollinger Bands show where price sits relative to its average. ATR answers a blunter question: how much does this thing actually move in a day right now?

True Range is the largest of three distances: today's high minus low, today's high minus yesterday's close, or today's low minus yesterday's close — capturing gaps, not just the visible daily range. ATR is simply a moving average (usually 14-period) of that value. Rising ATR means volatility is expanding; falling ATR means the market is going quiet. The practical payoff: size a stop as a multiple of ATR (say, entry minus 1.5×ATR) instead of a fixed dollar or point distance, so the stop automatically widens in a wild market and tightens in a calm one.

Explain it like I'm 5

ATR is like a gauge for how bumpy the road is today. Using the same seatbelt tightness every single day is fine on a smooth road but jerks you around on a bumpy one. ATR lets you loosen or tighten the "seatbelt" — the stop distance — to match how bumpy the ride actually is right now.

Sa Tagalog: Ang ATR parang panukat kung gaano ka-bumpy ang daan ngayon. Ang paggamit ng parehong higpit ng seatbelt araw-araw ay maayos sa tahimik na daan pero nakakayanig sa daanang bumpy. Hinahayaan ka ng ATR na iayon ang higpit ng "seatbelt" — ang layo ng stop — batay sa kung gaano ka-bumpy talaga ang byahe ngayon.
Field noteATR tells you how far, never which direction. Pair it with Section 04's structure to decide where the stop goes, and use ATR to sanity-check whether that distance is unusually tight or loose for current conditions.

Pivot Points

A support/resistance grid the entire market calculates the exact same way overnight — no drawing required.

Classic floor-trader pivots are derived purely from the previous session's high, low, and close: the Pivot (P) is their average, and a standard formula fans out Resistance 1–3 above it and Support 1–3 below. Because every charting platform computes the identical numbers from the identical prior-session data, these levels become natural high-attention zones for the same reason Fibonacci levels do (Section 11) — not magic, just mass agreement.

Explain it like I'm 5

Imagine every student in class independently does the same math homework from last night's numbers and somehow gets the exact same answer, without comparing notes. Since everyone lands on the same number anyway, that number becomes a natural meeting point — that's a pivot level.

Sa Tagalog: Isipin mo bawat estudyante sa klase ay hiwalay na kinakalkula ang parehong takdang-aral mula sa numero kagabi, at pareho ang nakukuhang sagot, kahit hindi sila nag-usap. Dahil pareho ang narating ng lahat, nagiging natural na lugar ng pagtatagpo ang numerong iyon — iyon ang pivot level.
Field notePivots reset every session and need no chart-drawing at all — a fast way to sketch a first map of the day before layering on the structure and volume tools from earlier sections.

Stop-Loss Placement

Where the stop actually goes matters more than most of the indicators in this notebook combined — and it's rarely just "a round number that feels safe."

Three common approaches, in ascending order of how much they actually listen to the chart. Percentage-based: a fixed % of price or account, simplest but blind to both structure and volatility — the weakest of the three. Volatility-based: a multiple of ATR (Section 33) from entry, adapting to current conditions. Structure-based: beyond the nearest swing high/low or S/R zone (Section 04) — ideally beyond the resting liquidity from Section 05 too, so an ordinary stop-hunt wick doesn't clip you before the real move. Let structure set the invalidation price first, then use position sizing (Section 17) to make that distance fit your 1% risk — never shrink the stop just to fit a position size you'd already decided on.

Explain it like I'm 5

Choosing a stop is like picking where to park a getaway car. Too close to the action and you get caught in the commotion. Too far and you waste time and effort getting back to it. The right spot is just past the danger zone — close enough to react fast, far enough to actually be safe from normal chaos.

Sa Tagalog: Ang pagpili ng stop ay parang pagpili kung saan iparada ang get-away car. Kung sobrang lapit sa aksyon, madadamay ka sa gulo. Kung sobrang layo, sayang lakas at oras pabalik. Ang tamang lugar ay bahagyang lampas sa panganib — malapit para makareact agad, malayo para talagang ligtas sa normal na kaguluhan.
Field noteIf a stop placed where structure says it should go makes the position "too small to bother with," the fix is a smaller position or a different setup — never a tighter stop just to make the math feel better.

Take-Profit & Exit Strategies

Entries get all the attention in this notebook so far. Exits decide the actual paycheck.

A fixed R:R target closes the whole position at a pre-planned multiple of risk (say, 2R) — simple and unemotional, but it caps a trend that keeps running. A structure-based target aims for the next opposing S/R zone or liquidity pool instead of an arbitrary multiple. A trailing stop — walking the stop up behind each new higher low, or trailing by a multiple of ATR — lets a strong trend run further than any fixed target would allow, at the cost of giving back some open profit on the way out. Scaling out takes partial profit at a first target and lets the remainder ride with a trailing stop toward a further one — banking certainty while keeping exposure to a bigger move.

Explain it like I'm 5

Exiting a trade is like harvesting fruit from a growing tree. Picking everything at one fixed height is simple, but you'll leave the tallest fruit unpicked if the tree keeps growing. A trailing stop is like raising your ladder step by step as the tree grows — you bank what's already in reach while still chasing what's still growing. Scaling out is picking half now and coming back for the rest later.

Sa Tagalog: Ang paglabas sa trade ay parang pag-ani ng prutas mula sa lumalaking puno. Ang pagpitas lahat sa parehong taas ay simple, pero maiiwan ang pinakamataas na prutas kung tumataas pa ang puno. Ang trailing stop ay parang unti-unting pagtaas ng hagdan habang lumalaki ang puno. Ang scaling out ay pagpitas ng kalahati ngayon at pagbalik para sa natitira mamaya.
Field notePick the exit style before the entry, not during it — deciding how you'll get out while the position is already open, and moving, is exactly the moment Section 16's psychology chapter warns you least trust yourself.

Bitcoin Halving & Long-Term Cycles

Every four years, the reward for mining a new Bitcoin block cuts in half — a supply shock written directly into the code, on a schedule nobody can change.

Roughly every 210,000 blocks (about four years), Bitcoin's issuance rate halves — the most recent halving, in 2024, cut the block reward from 6.25 to 3.125 BTC. Across the four halvings so far, long-term price cycles have loosely tracked this schedule: a quiet accumulation stretch, a rally over the following 12–18 months, then a multi-month drawdown before the next cycle begins. That is a historical pattern from a small number of cycles, not a guarantee — each cycle has actually differed in length and size from the ones before it.

Explain it like I'm 5

Imagine a farm that's only legally allowed to harvest half as much grain every four years. As less new supply reaches the market over time, if demand keeps up or grows, that shrinking supply has historically (not always) lined up with big price moves.

Sa Tagalog: Isipin mo bukid na pinapayagan lang mag-ani ng kalahati na lang ng dating dami tuwing apat na taon, ayon sa batas. Habang lumiliit ang bagong supply na dumarating sa merkado, kung tumataas o tumutuloy ang demand, madalas (hindi palagi) itong sumasabay sa malalaking galaw ng presyo.
Field noteFour halvings is a small sample size to call a law of nature. Use this as long-term context for your bias (Section 22), never as a precise entry or exit trigger by itself.

Futures Tax Basics

The tax authority treats regulated futures differently from most other trading in the US — worth knowing before year-end, not after.

In the US, regulated futures like CME Bitcoin futures generally count as Section 1256 contracts, which get a blanket 60/40 tax treatment: 60% of gains or losses taxed at the lower long-term capital gains rate and 40% at the short-term rate — regardless of how long the position was actually held, unlike stocks. Section 1256 contracts are also marked to market at year-end: open positions are treated as if closed at year-end fair value for tax purposes, even if you're still holding them. That's meaningfully different from spot crypto, which is taxed purely on actual holding period like any other property.

Sa Tagalog: Parang may special na toll lane sa highway na palaging pareho ang blended rate na singil kahit gaano kahaba o kaikli ang byahe, habang iba naman ang bayad sa regular na lane depende sa haba ng byahe. May sariling "blended rate" na lane ang mga futures contract, ayon sa batas.
Field noteThis is general education, not tax advice — treatment varies by country and changes over time. Confirm specifics with a licensed tax professional before you file, especially your first year trading futures.

Open Interest

Volume tells you how many contracts changed hands today. Open Interest tells you how many are still sitting open right now.

Open Interest (OI) is a running balance of outstanding futures contracts — it rises when a fresh buyer and fresh seller both open new positions, and falls when an existing long and existing short both close out. Unlike volume, it doesn't reset each day. Reading price alongside OI: price up with OI up means new longs are actually driving the move — a healthier signal than price up with OI down, which usually means short sellers are just covering (buying back to close), not new buyers stepping in. The same logic mirrors on the way down.

DownDownLongs closing out — possible exhaustion
Explain it like I'm 5

Volume is like counting every bet placed at a betting shop today, including ones already cashed out. Open interest is how many bets are still open and unsettled right now. If the number of open bets keeps growing, fresh money keeps entering the game. If it shrinks while price still moves, the old bettors are just leaving — nobody new is showing up.

Sa Tagalog: Ang volume ay parang pagbilang ng lahat ng pusta ngayong araw, kasama na yung na-cash out na. Ang open interest ay kung ilang pusta ang bukas pa at hindi pa nase-settle. Kung patuloy na tumataas ang bukas na pusta, may bagong perang pumapasok. Kung bumababa habang gumagalaw pa ang presyo, yung mga luma lang ang umaalis — walang bagong dumarating.
Field noteA breakout on rising volume (Section 07) and rising open interest is a stronger version of the same signal — it means real new participants are entering, not just existing positions swapping hands.

Contango, Backwardation & the Basis

CME BTC1! rarely trades at the exact same price as spot Bitcoin — the gap between them is called the basis, and its direction says something.

Basis = futures price minus spot price. Contango is futures trading above spot (positive basis) — the normal state for most of Bitcoin futures' history, reflecting financing cost and general bullish expectation. Backwardation is futures trading below spot (negative basis) — rarer, and often a sign of unusual near-term stress or heavy demand to short the futures contract specifically. Because a futures contract must converge exactly to spot at expiration, the basis mechanically shrinks toward zero as expiration approaches — the same convergence that drives the contract rollover in Section 14.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like preordering a concert ticket months early versus buying at the door tonight. The preorder ("futures") usually costs a bit more than today's door price ("spot") because of anticipation and the cost of holding the reservation — but by the actual night of the concert, both prices have to match exactly.

Sa Tagalog: Parang pag-preorder ng ticket ng konsyerto ilang buwan bago, kumpara sa pagbili sa pinto mismong gabi. Karaniwang mas mahal ang preorder ("futures") kaysa sa presyo ngayon ("spot") dahil sa inaasahan at gastos ng paghawak ng reserbasyon — pero pagdating ng aktwal na gabi, kailangang magkapareho na ang dalawang presyo.
Field noteThis is also why a fresh quarterly contract can open at a slightly different price than the one it replaces during rollover (Section 14) — it's pricing in a different amount of time until its own convergence date.

CME Price Limits & Circuit Breakers

Unlike spot crypto, CME Bitcoin futures can hit a wall mid-move — a built-in speed bump spot markets don't have.

CME futures markets carry exchange-mandated price limits: if price moves too far too fast from a reference level, trading can be temporarily restricted to a narrower range or paused outright, giving the market a cooling-off period before resuming. Spot crypto exchanges have no equivalent — they can move without limit straight through a panic, 24/7. The practical implication: in a genuinely extreme event, a stop-loss sitting in the futures market could fail to fill at the expected price if a limit triggers, or your position could simply be stuck until trading resumes.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like a highway that automatically closes for a few minutes if traffic starts moving dangerously fast in one direction, giving everyone a chance to slow down before it reopens — versus a road with no speed limit and no ability to close at all.

Sa Tagalog: Parang highway na kusang nagsasara ng ilang minuto kapag sobrang bilis ng takbo ng trapiko sa isang direksyon, para makapaghinahon ang lahat bago muling buksan — kumpara sa kalsadang walang speed limit at hindi kayang isara kahit kailan.
Field noteThis is a real, if rare, reason a stop-loss can behave unexpectedly on CME futures specifically — one more argument for the position-sizing math in Section 17 rather than assuming any single stop will always fill exactly where you drew it.

Options Basics & Hedging

CME also lists options on Bitcoin futures — a different tool for a different job: limiting downside without necessarily closing the trade.

A call option gives the right (not the obligation) to buy the futures contract at a set strike price before expiration; a put gives the right to sell. Buying a put against an existing long futures position is a classic hedge: if price falls, the put gains value to offset the futures loss, and the most you can lose on the hedge itself is capped at the premium you paid for it. Unlike a stop-loss, which exits the position entirely and can be hunted (Section 05), a protective put stays in place through a temporary wick and simply costs a known amount upfront.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like buying travel insurance for a trip you already booked. You still keep the trip (the futures position), but if something goes wrong, the insurance (the put) pays out to offset the damage — and the most you can lose is the cost of the insurance itself.

Sa Tagalog: Parang pagbili ng travel insurance para sa biyaheng na-book mo na. Tuloy pa rin ang biyahe (ang futures position), pero kung may masamang mangyari, magbabayad ang insurance (ang put) para matakpan ang pinsala — at ang pinakamalaki mong malulugi ay ang bayad sa insurance mismo.
Field noteOptions pricing has its own vocabulary — premium, strike, expiration, implied volatility — well beyond one page. Treat this section as knowing the concept exists, not a complete options education.

Backtesting Walkthrough

Section 21 gave you the expectancy formula. This is how you actually go get the numbers to put into it.

Write the exact rule set down first — entry trigger, stop rule, exit rule — vague rules produce unreliable results. Then scroll a chart backward in time (or use a proper replay tool) so you genuinely can't see what happens next. Move forward one candle at a time, marking every instance the entry rule would have triggered, and record entry, stop, and exit for each one exactly per the written rules. After 30–50-plus instances, compute win rate and average R the same way Section 21 does. The biggest threat is hindsight bias — it's easy to unconsciously only "notice" the setups that would have worked when scanning a finished chart. Disciplined bar-by-bar replay is what keeps a backtest honest.

$ backtest --rules written --bars 1D
[ ] entry / stop / exit rules written down, no ambiguity
[ ] replaying bar-by-bar, not scanning the finished chart
[ ] every triggered instance logged, win or lose
[ ] 30+ instances before trusting the win rate
[ ] win rate & avg R computed — run Section 21's formula
Explain it like I'm 5

It's like practicing free throws with a version of yourself who doesn't know yet if the ball goes in. Testing a rule by scrolling forward one moment at a time — instead of looking at the whole finished chart first — is the only way to know if the rule really works, rather than just noticing the shots that happened to land.

Sa Tagalog: Parang nagsasanay ng free throw gamit ang bersyon ng sarili mo na hindi pa alam kung papasok ang bola. Ang pagsubok sa rule sa pamamagitan ng dahan-dahang pag-scroll pasulong — hindi pagtingin agad sa buong tapos nang chart — ang tanging paraan para malaman kung talagang gumagana ito, hindi lang napansin ang mga pagkakataong pumasok.
Field noteLog backtested trades in the same format as live demo trades in Section 26 — keeping the format identical makes it trivial to compare backtested expectancy against what you're actually producing live.

Market Makers & the Other Side of Your Trade

Every position has a counterparty. On a regulated futures exchange, professional market makers are often exactly that.

Market makers are firms that continuously post both a bid and an ask (Section 20), profiting from the spread rather than betting on direction — their job is to always be willing to trade, providing the liquidity retail orders fill against. That's different from a large directional trader deliberately hunting a stop (Section 05). Much of the routine order-filling on the other side of a small trade is just market makers doing their job, not a conspiracy. Practically, this reframes the "liquidity grab" language: sometimes a wick beyond a level is a deliberate hunt, sometimes it's just market-maker quotes reacting mechanically to a burst of orders — either way, the response is the same: respect the zone, don't take it personally.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like a lemonade stand owner who's happy to both buy your empty cups back and sell you lemonade all day, earning a small profit on the difference each time, no matter whether lemonade prices are rising or falling. They're not betting on where lemonade is headed — they profit just from always being available on both sides.

Sa Tagalog: Parang tindero ng lemonade na masaya bumili pabalik ng basyong baso mo at magbenta rin ng lemonade sa iyo buong araw, kumikita ng kaunti sa pagkakaiba kada beses, tumaas man o bumaba ang presyo ng lemonade ngayon. Hindi sila nagtataya kung saan patutungo ang presyo — kumikita lang sila dahil laging bukas sila sa magkabilang panig.
Field noteThis doesn't mean liquidity grabs (Section 05) aren't real — it means not every wick beyond a level is a deliberate hunt. Judge by what happens next (a hard reversal that holds, versus a tag-and-continue) rather than assuming intent.

Stock Market Basics

Everything about candles, structure, and indicators so far transfers directly to stocks. What's actually different is the plumbing underneath.

Stocks trade on centralized exchanges (NYSE, Nasdaq) during a regular session (9:30am–4:00pm ET), bracketed by thinner pre-market and after-hours windows where far fewer participants means wider spreads (Section 20) and choppier moves. Stocks carry their own recurring volatility events beyond the general news calendar (Section 23): earnings reports, released quarterly and often causing the single biggest move of a stock's quarter; dividends, which cause a small, predictable gap down on the ex-dividend date roughly equal to the payout; and stock splits, which change the share count and price without changing the company's actual value.

One US-specific rule with no equivalent in futures or forex: the Pattern Day Trader (PDT) rule. An account under $25,000 in a margin account is restricted to three day-trades per rolling five-business-day period — a hard constraint worth knowing before funding a small stock account for active trading.

Explain it like I'm 5

The stock market is like a store with official opening hours, plus a side door open a bit earlier and later for early or late shoppers. Fewer staff and shoppers use that side door, so prices can swing more per purchase. The PDT rule is like a library card that only lets you borrow three books in any five-day stretch unless you keep a bigger deposit on file — and that rule only applies to this particular library, not the bookstore next door.

Sa Tagalog: Ang stock market ay parang tindahan na may opisyal na oras ng pagbukas, may side door na bukas nang mas maaga o mas huli para sa unang o huling mamimili. Konti lang ang tauhan at customer doon, kaya mas malaki ang galaw ng presyo kada bili. Ang PDT rule ay parang library card na tatlong libro lang ang pwedeng hiramin sa loob ng limang araw maliban kung mas malaki ang deposito mo — at ang tuntuning ito ay sa librong ito lang, wala nito ang tindahan sa kabila.
Field noteAn earnings report is the stock equivalent of Section 23's news events, except it's scheduled quarterly for every single stock you might hold — check the date before assuming a quiet chart will stay quiet.

Forex Market Basics

No single exchange, no closing bell — just banks and brokers trading currency against currency, nearly 24 hours a day, five days a week.

A currency pair like EUR/USD quotes the base currency (EUR) against the quote currency (USD). Majors pair a big economy's currency with the US dollar (EUR/USD, GBP/USD, USD/JPY); minors or cross pairs skip the dollar entirely (EUR/GBP); exotics pair a major currency with an emerging-market one, and trade with wider spreads and thinner liquidity. Forex measures movement in pips — typically the fourth decimal place (the second, for JPY pairs) — and sizes positions in lots: a standard lot is 100,000 units of the base currency, a mini lot 10,000, a micro lot 1,000.

Forex has no central exchange at all — it's purely over-the-counter, a global network of banks and brokers acting as market makers (Section 45) for each other. That structure is also why the Section 29 session overlaps matter more here than almost anywhere else: the London/New York overlap is the single biggest volume window in the entire forex day. One forex-specific fundamental idea worth knowing: the carry trade — holding a currency with a high interest rate funded by borrowing one with a low rate, earning the rate difference as long as the exchange rate itself doesn't move against you enough to erase it.

Explain it like I'm 5

A pip is like counting in cents instead of dollars — a currency pair rarely moves by a whole "dollar" at once, so traders zoom into a smaller unit to measure movement that actually matters. A carry trade is like borrowing money from a friend who barely charges interest, then lending it to another friend who pays you well — you pocket the difference, as long as nothing else changes.

Sa Tagalog: Ang pip ay parang pagbilang sa sentimo imbes na piso — bihirang gumalaw nang buong "piso" ang isang currency pair, kaya sinusukat ng mga trader ang mas maliit na yunit na talagang mahalaga. Ang carry trade ay parang panghihiram ng pera sa kaibigang halos walang singil na interes, tapos pinapautang mo sa ibang kaibigang mataas ang bayad na interes — kikitain mo ang pagkakaiba, hangga't walang ibang nagbabago.
Field noteBecause forex has no single exchange, "the market" you see is really your broker's own quotes — spreads (Section 20) and even prices can differ slightly from one broker to the next, unlike a centralized exchange like CME.

Short Selling & Borrow Mechanics

Going short in stocks isn't just "the opposite of long" — you're borrowing something you don't own, and that borrowing has its own costs and risks.

To short a stock, a broker lends you shares (borrowed from another client's holdings) which you immediately sell; later you buy them back ("cover") and return them, profiting if the price fell. Unlike futures, where long and short are perfectly symmetric contract positions, stock shorting requires shares to actually be borrowable — popular short targets can become "hard to borrow," carrying a fee for as long as the position stays open, or become unavailable entirely. Risk is theoretically unlimited: a stock can keep rising indefinitely, unlike falling to zero being the floor for a long. A short squeeze happens when a heavily-shorted stock rallies hard, forcing shorts to buy back to cover — adding fuel to the very rally that's hurting them.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like borrowing a friend's bicycle to sell today, planning to buy an identical one back later, cheaper, and return it — profiting from the price drop. But if that bike suddenly becomes rare and expensive, you could end up paying way more than you sold it for, with no real ceiling on how bad it gets.

Sa Tagalog: Parang paghiram ng bisikleta ng kaibigan mo para ibenta ngayon, may plano bumili ng kaparehong bisikleta mamaya nang mas mura at ibalik sa kanya — kikita ka sa pagbaba ng presyo. Pero kung biglang naging bihira at mahal bilhin ulit ang bisikleta, baka kailanganin mong magbayad nang sobrang laki, at walang tunay na "kisame" kung gaano kalala maging ito.
Field noteA short squeeze is Section 05's liquidity grab wearing a different costume — stops and forced buybacks stacked above a heavily-shorted price level, ready to be triggered.

Beta & Sector Correlation

Not every stock move is about that company — sometimes it's just the whole market, or the whole sector, dragging it along.

Beta measures how much a stock tends to move relative to a broad index like the S&P 500 — a beta of 1.5 means it tends to move about 1.5× as much as the index, same direction; a beta near zero means it moves mostly independently. Stocks in the same sector (all banks, all semiconductor makers) often move together because they share the same macro exposures — interest rates for banks, chip demand cycles for semis. Checking a sector ETF before trading an individual stock in that sector can matter as much as the stock's own chart. Diversifying across genuinely uncorrelated names reduces risk far more than owning many stocks that are secretly all high-beta and same-sector.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like a flock of birds flying in formation — most turn together when the leader (the market or sector) turns, even if a couple of birds have their own slightly exaggerated wobble (higher beta). Watching just one bird without watching the flock's direction misses most of the story.

Sa Tagalog: Parang grupo ng ibong lumilipad nang magkasama — karamihan ay lumiliko nang sabay kapag lumiko ang pinuno (ang market o sector), kahit may ilang ibong mas malaki ang sariling wobble (mas mataas na beta). Ang panonood lang sa isang ibon nang hindi tinitingnan ang direksyon ng buong flock ay nawawala ang karamihan ng kwento.
Field noteThis is Section 22's macro correlation idea, scaled down from "the whole market" to "this one sector" — the same instinct to zoom out before trusting a single chart.

IPOs & New Listings

A stock with no trading history yet breaks almost every rule in this notebook — there's no chart to read until one exists.

An IPO (Initial Public Offering) is a company's first sale of shares to the public; a SPAC is an alternate route to going public by merging into an already-listed shell company. Freshly listed stocks have no meaningful support/resistance history (Section 04) and no established average-volume baseline (Section 07), so early price discovery can be extremely volatile with no anchor. A lock-up period (commonly 90–180 days) restricts insiders from selling immediately after listing — its expiration is a scheduled, predictable event that can add sudden new selling pressure, in the same spirit as an earnings date (Section 46) but specific to newly public companies.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like moving into a brand new subdivision with no map yet — everyone's still figuring out which streets are good and which aren't, so the "rules of the road" that work in an established neighborhood don't quite apply yet.

Sa Tagalog: Parang paglipat sa bagong subdivision na wala pang mapa — nagbabahagi pa ang lahat kung saan ang magaganda at hindi magandang kalye, kaya hindi pa masyadong angkop ang mga "tuntunin sa daan" na gumagana sa lumang subdivision.
Field noteMost of this notebook assumes an established chart with real history behind it. A stock with only a few months of trading history is the exception — treat the first pattern you see on it with real suspicion.

ETFs: Trading a Whole Market at Once

An ETF lets you buy an entire basket of stocks, a sector, or even a strategy, in one single trade.

An Exchange-Traded Fund (ETF) holds a basket of underlying assets — SPY holds all S&P 500 stocks — and trades on an exchange just like a single stock, with its own live price, volume, and chart that every tool in this notebook applies to directly. ETFs exist for broad indices, specific sectors, commodities, bonds, and even leveraged or inverse strategies that amplify or invert the underlying's daily move. Trading an ETF is a way to express a view on an entire sector or market (tying back to Section 50's beta idea) without taking on one company's individual risk.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like buying a fruit basket instead of picking one single fruit — you get a mix, so one bad piece doesn't ruin everything, but you also don't get the full upside if just one fruit turns out amazing.

Sa Tagalog: Parang pagbili ng basket ng prutas imbes na isang piraso lang — may pagkakahalo-halo ka, kaya hindi masisira ng isang masamang piraso ang lahat, pero hindi mo rin makukuha ang buong benepisyo kung isang prutas lang pala ang naging sobrang sarap.
Field noteA 2x leveraged ETF does not simply double the underlying's return over weeks or months — it resets daily, and that compounding can produce surprising results in a choppy, sideways market. Treat these as short-term tools, not buy-and-hold positions.

Central Bank Policy & Key Economic Indicators

In forex, one institution's decision can move a currency more in ten minutes than a week of chart patterns.

Central banks — the Federal Reserve for USD, the ECB for EUR, the BOJ for JPY — set the interest rate that anchors their currency's value. Higher rates generally attract capital seeking yield, strengthening a currency (tying to Section 47's carry trade); rate cuts do the opposite. Scheduled rate decisions and the "forward guidance" commentary that comes with them are among the highest-impact events in forex, often dwarfing the generic news events already covered in Section 23. Key indicators that feed into those decisions and move currencies on their own release: CPI (inflation), GDP (growth), and PMI (a forward-looking survey of business activity) — each released on a regular calendar, per country.

Currency Correlation

Trading three "different" pairs that are all secretly the same bet is a common way to accidentally triple your risk.

Some currency pairs move in a strong, persistent relationship. EUR/USD and GBP/USD often move together — both simply reflect "USD weaker" or "USD stronger." AUD/USD and NZD/USD often correlate closely, sharing similar commodity-linked economies. Trading multiple positions that are all secretly betting on the same underlying move multiplies real exposure far beyond what sizing each trade individually suggests — the position-sizing math from Section 17 assumes independent trades, and correlated positions quietly break that assumption.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like thinking you spread your bets across three different horses in a race, but two of the "different" horses are actually litter-mates that always run at almost the exact same pace — you didn't really diversify, you just bet three times on the same outcome.

Sa Tagalog: Parang akala mo nag-iba-iba ka ng pusta sa tatlong magkaibang kabayo sa karera, pero dalawa sa mga "magkaibang" kabayo pala ay magkapatid na palaging halos magkasabay tumakbo — hindi mo talaga na-diversify, tatlong beses ka lang nagpusta sa parehong resulta.
Field noteBefore stacking multiple forex positions, check whether they're actually independent bets or the same bet wearing different tickers — Section 17's 1% rule assumes risk doesn't stack silently like this.

Prop Firms & Funded Accounts

A prop firm lets you trade their capital instead of your own — for a fee, and under rules stricter than any broker's default settings.

A funded-account program typically runs two phases. The evaluation (or "challenge") has you trade under specific profit targets and drawdown limits to prove your process, on a fee you pay upfront. Pass, and the funded phase allocates capital and pays you a split of profits — often 70–90% to the trader — as long as you keep following the rules. Common ways to fail: exceeding a maximum daily loss, exceeding the overall maximum drawdown, or holding through a restricted news event the firm prohibits. The appeal is trading with more size than a small personal account could support; the cost is the evaluation fee itself, usually non-refundable on a fail — and a business model where the firm profits partly from failed evaluations, not only successful traders.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like auditioning for a chauffeur job by driving a rental car under strict rules first — don't speed, don't run red lights, park exactly right. Pass the audition and they hand you the keys to a real car and pay you to drive it, keeping a cut of what you earn.

Sa Tagalog: Parang audition para sa trabahong chauffeur — nagmamaneho ka muna ng rental car sa ilalim ng mahigpit na tuntunin (huwag bumilis, huwag lumagpas sa pulang ilaw, tamang pagpark). Kapag pumasa ka, ibibigay sa iyo ang susi ng tunay na kotse at babayaran ka para magmaneho, may hawak silang bahagi ng kikitain mo.
Field noteA prop firm's daily-loss and max-drawdown rules are just someone else's version of Section 17's 1% rule, enforced with a hard cutoff instead of self-discipline. Failing an evaluation under those rules is useful information about your own process either way.

Slippage & Execution Quality

The price you see on the screen and the price you actually get filled at are not always the same number.

Slippage is the gap between the expected price of a trade and the price it actually executes at. It shows up most in fast-moving or thin markets — a market order during a news spike (Section 23) or a CME gap open (Section 08) can fill several points worse than the last quoted price, because the order "walks" through several levels of the order book (Section 20) before it's fully filled. Limit orders avoid price slippage but risk not filling at all. Expect wider effective costs around scheduled high-impact events, and size positions knowing a stop might fill worse than its exact drawn price in extreme conditions — the same concern behind Section 42's price limits.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like ordering the last known price of a popular item online, but by the time your order actually processes, the cheap stock sold out and you get charged the next-tier price instead. The gap between what you expected and what you actually paid is the slippage.

Sa Tagalog: Parang nag-order ka online sa huling nakitang presyo ng paborito mong produkto, pero pagdating ng checkout, naubos na yung mura at na-charge ka na sa susunod na mas mataas na presyo. Ang agwat sa pagitan ng inaasahan mo at ang aktwal na binayaran mo ang slippage.
Field noteThis is exactly why Section 14 flagged market orders as fastest but riskiest on thin liquidity — slippage is that risk showing up as an actual number on your fill.

The Pattern Day Trader (PDT) Rule

In the US, a stock account under $25,000 can get flagged and frozen for trading too actively — a rule that doesn't exist for the futures account this notebook started on.

FINRA's Pattern Day Trader rule applies to margin accounts trading US stocks: execute 4 or more day trades (opening and closing the same position within one day) within any 5 rolling business days, and if those trades are more than 6% of your total activity in that window, the account gets flagged as PDT — which then requires at least $25,000 in equity to keep day trading, or the account gets restricted from day trading entirely. This doesn't apply to futures accounts like the CME BTC1! chart this notebook started on, or to cash accounts that skip margin (though cash accounts carry their own settlement-timing restrictions instead). A small stock account has to either trade less often, accept a cash account's limitations, or trade an instrument that doesn't carry this restriction at all.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like a library that lets you borrow books freely, but if you check out and return more than 3 books the same day, too many times in a week, they start requiring a bigger deposit before letting you keep doing that — a rule that only applies to that one library's system, not a different library across town with different rules.

Sa Tagalog: Parang aklatan na malayang nagpapahiram ng libro, pero kung sobra ka nang mag-checkout at magsauli ng higit sa 3 libro sa parehong araw, paulit-ulit sa loob ng isang linggo, hihingan ka nila ng mas malaking deposito — tuntunin na para lang sa aklatang iyon, hindi sa ibang aklatan sa kabilang bayan na may ibang patakaran.
Field noteThis is exactly the kind of account-rule difference Section 48 warned about — the chart-reading skills transfer between stocks and futures, this specific restriction does not.

Trading Psychology: Demo to Live

Everything in Section 16 is true on a demo account. Real money adds one variable no demo can simulate: it actually hurts to lose it.

A concrete graduation protocol: don't go live until backtested-plus-demo expectancy (Sections 21 and 44) is consistently positive across 50-plus instances. When you do go live, size at 10–25% of what you'd normally risk for the first month — specifically to practice executing the plan under real emotion, not to maximize returns yet. Expect execution to get worse before it gets better: hesitating on entries you'd have taken on demo, or exiting early out of fear, is the normal pattern the tiny size exists to surface cheaply. Only scale size back up once live execution actually matches demo execution — not before.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like a pilot who trained perfectly in a flight simulator, then flies their first real solo flight with an instructor sitting right there, in deliberately calm weather. Not because the simulator lied, but because real flight adds real consequences that need to be proven out gradually, somewhere lower-stakes, before flying commercial passengers.

Sa Tagalog: Parang piloto na perpekto ang training sa flight simulator, pero sa unang tunay na solo flight niya, may kasamang instructor at sinasadyang kalmado ang panahon. Hindi dahil nagsinungaling ang simulator, kundi dahil ang tunay na paglipad ay may tunay na kahihinatnan na kailangang subukan nang unti-unti, sa mas ligtas na sitwasyon muna, bago magdala ng tunay na pasahero.
Field noteThis is the mentor letter's warning turned into an actual protocol. Skipping the tiny-size phase isn't efficiency — it's skipping the one experiment that tells you whether your plan survives contact with real fear and greed.

Commodity Futures Basics

BTC1! isn't the only future on your watchlist — gold and oil futures run on the same mechanics, with their own personalities.

Explain it like I'm 5

Different family members react differently to the same news at dinner. One relative (gold) gets cautious and hoards resources when there's bad news on TV. Another (oil) mostly reacts to whether the family farm actually produced more or less this season — not really to the news itself.

Fundamental Analysis Basics

Everything in this notebook so far reads price. This is a quick look at reading the actual business underneath a stock instead.

Fundamental analysis evaluates a company's underlying financial health rather than its chart. Beginner-level metrics worth knowing: the P/E ratio (price-to-earnings — how much investors pay per dollar of profit, useful for comparing similar companies, less useful alone); revenue and earnings growth (is the business actually growing); and debt levels (a heavily indebted company is more fragile in a downturn). None of this predicts tomorrow's candle — a great company can have an overextended, technically weak chart, and a struggling company can have a strong short-term technical setup. Most professional stock traders use both lenses together: fundamentals to decide what's worth watching at all, technicals to decide when and where to actually enter and exit.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like choosing a restaurant (fundamentals — is the food actually good, is the kitchen well-run) versus choosing exactly when to walk in and sit down (technicals — is there a table open right now). A great restaurant with a two-hour wait right now might not be worth entering yet; a mediocre restaurant with an open table isn't a good choice just because it's easy to walk into.

Sa Tagalog: Parang pagpili ng restaurant (fundamentals — masarap ba talaga ang pagkain, maayos ba ang kusina) kumpara sa eksaktong oras ng pagpasok (technicals — may bakanteng mesa ba ngayon). Ang magandang restaurant na may dalawang oras na paghihintay ngayon ay baka hindi pa dapat pasukan. Ang katamtamang restaurant na may bakanteng mesa ay hindi rin maganda kung dahil lang madaling pumasok.

Volume Profile & Value Area

Section 07's volume bars show how much traded over time. Volume Profile turns that sideways — showing how much traded at each price.

A Volume Profile plots volume horizontally against price for a chosen period, revealing where trading activity actually concentrated. The single price with the most volume is the Point of Control (POC). The Value Area — typically the range holding 70% of total volume — marks where consensus "fair value" formed. Price often gravitates back toward the POC after moving away, functioning as another support/resistance tool alongside pivots (Section 34) and Fibonacci (Section 11). Price that moves cleanly out of the value area on strong volume is often signaling a genuine shift in consensus, not just noise.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like looking at footprints in the sand after a full day at the beach. Instead of knowing when people walked by (volume over time), you can see where most people actually stood and gathered (volume by price) — that well-trodden spot is where the crowd agreed to hang out.

Sa Tagalog: Parang pagtingin sa bakas ng paa sa buhangin pagkatapos ng buong araw sa dalampasigan. Imbes na malaman kung KAILAN dumaan ang mga tao (volume sa paglipas ng oras), makikita mo kung SAAN talaga natipon ang karamihan (volume sa bawat presyo) — yung matapak na lugar ang pinagkasunduang tambayan ng grupo.
Field noteTreat the POC and value-area edges the same way Section 04 treats any support/resistance zone — worth watching, confirmed by what price actually does there, not a guarantee by itself.

Portfolio Heat: Sizing Across Multiple Trades

Section 17's 1% rule is about one trade. What happens when five are open at once?

Portfolio heat is the total percentage of an account at risk across every currently open position combined. Five separate trades each risking 1% sounds disciplined in isolation — but if several hit their stops the same day (which correlated positions, per Section 54, make more likely than expected), that's a real multi-percent hit at once, and if any of those positions are secretly correlated, the effective risk is higher than the simple sum suggests. A common professional guideline caps total portfolio heat somewhere around 3–6% across all open positions, sizing new trades down as more positions are already live.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like five people each deciding it's fine to carry one plate at a family buffet, not realizing the table itself has a weight limit. One plate each is fine — but if everyone piles on at once, the table can still collapse even though no single plate was too heavy.

Sa Tagalog: Parang limang tao na paisa-isang nagdesisyon na okay lang magdala ng isang plato sa buffet ng pamilya, hindi napapansin na may limitasyon din pala sa bigat ang mismong mesa. Okay ang isang plato bawat isa, pero kung sabay-sabay nilang ilalagay, pwede pa ring gumuho ang mesa kahit walang isang platong sobrang bigat.
Field noteBefore opening a new position, add up the risk on everything already open, accounting for Section 54's correlation check. The 1% rule per trade doesn't protect you if the total keeps stacking unchecked.

Personal Circuit Breakers

Section 42 covers the exchange's circuit breakers. This is about building your own, before the exchange's version ever needs to kick in.

A personal circuit breaker is a pre-committed rule that stops all trading once an account hits a set drawdown threshold — for example, "-5% in a week means stop until Monday and review," or "-10% in a month means back to demo until three consecutive profitable review weeks" (Section 39). Unlike the mentor letter's "two losses ends the day" rule, which catches a single bad session, this operates on a longer timeframe and catches the slower bleed of a strategy quietly not working, or a trader quietly drifting from their own plan, before it becomes a much bigger hole. The rule only works if it's decided in advance, in writing, while calm — not negotiated with yourself in the moment.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like a smoke detector that's set and tested before a fire ever starts. By the time you can smell smoke yourself, it's already a bigger problem than the alarm was built to catch early.

Sa Tagalog: Parang smoke detector na naka-set at nasubok na bago pa man magkaroon ng sunog. Sa oras na maaamoy mo na mismo ang usok, mas malaki na ang problema kaysa sa dapat sanang naabutan ng alarm nang maaga.
Field noteWrite your specific thresholds into the Section 25 trading plan now, while calm. A circuit breaker decided mid-drawdown isn't a circuit breaker — it's just another emotional decision wearing a rule's costume.

Trading vs. Investing: Two Different Games

Every technique in this notebook assumes you're trading. Quietly switching to "investing" mid-trade is one of the most common ways a small loss becomes a large one.

Trading and investing are different games with different time horizons and different definitions of success. Trading — the entire focus of this notebook — works on technical structure, a defined stop, and a plan measured in days to months. Investing works on business fundamentals (Section 60), a horizon of years, and deliberately rides out volatility that would stop a trade out. The trap: a trade goes against plan, the stop should have triggered, and the trader tells themselves "I'll just hold this long-term now" — retroactively reclassifying a failed trade as an investment to avoid admitting the loss. That isn't investing. It's Section 16's sunk-cost holding wearing a respectable label.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like calling a wrong turn on a road trip "just a scenic route" instead of admitting you took a wrong turn. Relabeling it doesn't change that you're now somewhere you didn't plan to be, with different costs than if you'd meant to go there from the start.

Sa Tagalog: Parang tinawag mong "scenic route lang" yung maling liko sa road trip, imbes na aminin na nagkamali ka ng liko. Hindi mababago ng panibagong pangalan na nasa lugar ka ngayon na hindi mo talaga plano puntahan, na may kaakibat na ibang gastos kaysa kung sinadya mo talagang pumunta doon.
Field noteIf a position only became a "long-term investment" after it started losing, it isn't one — it's Section 16's sunk-cost holding with a rebrand. Decide which game you're playing before the trade, not after it goes wrong.

Margin Calls: The Actual Math

Section 18 explained that leverage amplifies moves. This is the exact arithmetic behind the moment a broker actually calls.

Two numbers matter: Initial Margin (the deposit required to open a leveraged position) and Maintenance Margin (the minimum equity required to keep it open, usually lower than initial margin). As a losing position's value falls, account equity falls with it; once equity drops below the maintenance margin level, a margin call triggers — a demand to deposit more funds immediately, or the position gets force-liquidated by the broker, often at a worse price than the trader would have chosen. Worked example: $1,000 initial margin on a position with a $700 maintenance requirement — a loss that brings account equity down to $699 triggers the call, well before the account reaches zero.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like a landlord who doesn't wait for you to owe zero dollars before acting. The moment your deposit on file drops below an agreed minimum cushion, they call demanding you top it back up immediately — well before you're actually broke.

Sa Tagalog: Parang panginoong-maylupa na hindi naghihintay hanggang maubos ang lahat ng pera mo bago kumilos. Sa sandaling bumaba ang naka-deposito mo sa ibaba ng napagkasunduang minimum na kolintahan, tatawagan ka niya agad para dagdagan ito, bago ka pa man talagang mawalan ng lahat.
Field noteThis is exactly why Section 17's position sizing matters more than Section 18's leverage limit alone — correct sizing keeps a normal losing streak from ever getting near the maintenance margin line in the first place.

Perpetual Futures & Funding Rate

Everything in this notebook about CME futures assumes a real expiration date. Crypto exchanges also list a contract with no expiration at all — kept near spot by a fee traders pay each other instead.

A perpetual futures contract ("perp"), common on crypto exchanges, behaves like a futures contract (Section 14) that never expires — it can be held indefinitely. Without an expiration date to force convergence with spot the way Section 41's basis does, a perp needs a different mechanism to stay near the spot price: the funding rate. At each funding interval (commonly every 8 hours), whichever side of the contract is trading at a premium pays a small fee directly to the other side. When the perp trades above spot — longs more aggressive — longs pay shorts; when it trades below spot, shorts pay longs. A sustained, unusually high positive funding rate is itself worth reading as sentiment: it means the crowd is heavily, expensively long, and a sharp de-leveraging move against that crowded positioning becomes more likely, not less.

Explain it like I'm 5

A perpetual contract is like a rental with no end date, but the rent keeps adjusting to match what a similar unit nearby (spot) actually costs. If way more people want in on a unit like yours than the going rate justifies, you as the long pay a small top-up to whoever's on the other side of the lease, every few hours, to keep the price honest.

Sa Tagalog: Ang perpetual contract ay parang paupahan na walang katapusang petsa, pero patuloy na inaayos ang upa para tumapat sa presyo ng katulad na paupahan sa kalapit (spot). Kung mas marami ang gustong umupa katulad ng sa iyo kaysa sa dapat, ikaw bilang umuupa ang magbabayad ng maliit na dagdag sa kabilang panig, tuwing ilang oras, para mapanatiling tapat ang presyo.
Field noteThe CME contract this notebook is built on has an expiration date and rolls (Section 14) instead of a funding rate — it's a genuinely different mechanic, not just a cosmetic one. Before holding a perpetual swap through multiple funding intervals, check the current rate; it's a real, recurring cost or income stacked on top of price movement.

Sentiment Extremes & the Fear/Greed Index

The crowd is usually right for most of a trend and most wrong right at its extremes — a handful of tools try to measure that crowd mood directly, as a number.

Sentiment indicators attempt to quantify crowd psychology (Section 16) instead of just describing it. A widely watched example in crypto is the Fear & Greed Index, which blends volatility, momentum, social activity, and other inputs into a single 0–100 reading — near 0 labeled "Extreme Fear," near 100 "Extreme Greed." The contrarian read many traders lean on: extreme greed often coincides with the late, crowded, over-leveraged stage of a rally (tying to Section 67's funding rate — extreme greed and extreme positive funding often show up together), and extreme fear often coincides with the capitulation Section 09's Wyckoff schematic describes. None of this is a timing tool on its own — a market can sit in "extreme greed" for weeks before actually turning, the same caution Section 12 gives RSI for staying "overbought."

Explain it like I'm 5

Think of it like a hallway thermometer for excitement before a big game. When almost everyone is shouting with total confidence that their team can't lose, that's usually not the calm, clear-headed moment it feels like. A sentiment extreme, in either direction, is a nudge to double-check your own thinking, not a scoreboard to trade off of directly.

Sa Tagalog: Isipin mo itong parang thermometer ng kaba sa pasilyo bago ang malaking laro. Kapag halos lahat ay sigaw nang sigaw na tiyak silang mananalo, karaniwan hindi iyon yung kalmado at malinaw na pag-iisip na pakiramdam nito. Ang sukdulang antas, sa alinmang direksyon, ay senyales para muling suriin ang sarili mong pag-iisip, hindi iskorbord na basehan agad ng desisyon.
Field noteTreat a sentiment extreme as one more input for Section 30's confluence check, not a standalone signal — pair it with actual structure, volume, and open interest before acting on a number that's ultimately just measuring how everyone else currently feels.

Stock Exchanges & Order Matching

Every stock trade needs a buyer and seller to actually meet at the same price, at the same instant — an exchange's whole job is making that happen, fairly and fast.

A stock exchange is fundamentally a matching engine: it collects every buy and sell order for a stock and pairs them using price-time priority — the best price gets matched first, and among orders at the same price, whoever placed theirs first gets filled first. NYSE historically ran a hybrid model with a designated market maker per stock, obligated to keep an orderly market; Nasdaq is a pure electronic dealer market where multiple market makers (Section 45) compete to post the best bid and ask (Section 20) for every stock. Modern orders also route across dozens of exchanges and dark pools — private venues that don't publicly display resting orders before execution — simultaneously; a broker's job is finding the best combined price and liquidity across all of them, a process called order routing.

Explain it like I'm 5

An exchange is like a really well-run ticket resale board: everyone posts what they'll pay or accept, and the board pairs the first person who agreed to a price with whoever is offering it, before looking at anyone else who showed up later wanting the same deal.

Sa Tagalog: Ang exchange ay parang maayos na resale board ng tiket — ipinopost ng lahat kung magkano ang gusto nilang bayaran o tanggapin, at pinapares ng board ang unang taong sumang-ayon sa presyo, bago tingnan pa ang ibang dumating na mas huli na gusto rin ng parehong deal.
Field noteYou never place an order "at NYSE" or "at Nasdaq" directly — your broker routes it, and Section 56's slippage often comes from exactly this fragmentation: your order working across multiple venues before it's fully filled.

Market Participants: Who's on the Other Side

Every trade has a counterparty — knowing who's typically on the other side changes how you read a chart.

Retail traders are individuals trading personal accounts — collectively significant in total volume but typically small in individual order size. Institutional investors — mutual funds, pension funds, insurance companies — manage large pools of capital and often must execute big orders slowly, over hours or days, to avoid moving price against themselves (tying to Section 05's liquidity idea — a large institutional order is often exactly what "grabs" liquidity through a level). Hedge funds trade more actively and can take both long and short positions with leverage. Market makers and high-frequency trading (HFT) firms (Section 45) provide continuous liquidity, profiting from the spread and tiny, extremely fast price differences, often holding positions for seconds or less.

Explain it like I'm 5

Picture a busy market: some people are shoppers buying a few items for dinner (retail), some run restaurants placing huge weekly orders (institutions), some flip goods fast for a quick profit (hedge funds), and some just stand at a stall all day quoting prices to everyone who walks by (market makers).

Primary vs Secondary Markets

The IPO is the only time a company itself actually gets your money. Every other trade in that stock's life is just investors trading with each other.

The primary market is where new shares are created and sold for the first time — an IPO (Section 51) or a follow-on offering, where the company receives the proceeds directly, diluting existing shares to raise cash. The secondary market is everywhere trading happens afterward: every trade on NYSE or Nasdaq is investors buying and selling existing shares among themselves (Section 69's matching engine) — the company itself gets nothing further from those trades, no matter how high the stock later climbs. A buyback works in reverse of a secondary offering: the company spends its own cash in the secondary market to retire shares, shrinking the float and, all else equal, increasing each remaining share's claim on the company.

Explain it like I'm 5

A band selling its own concert tickets for the first time is the primary market — they get that money. Every time a fan resells that ticket to another fan afterward is the secondary market — the band never sees another peso from those resales, no matter how high the resale price climbs.

Sa Tagalog: Ang unang beses na nagbenta ang isang banda ng sarili nilang tiket sa konsyerto ay ang primary market — sila ang kumikita noon. Tuwing ibinebenta ng isang fan sa kapwa fan ang tiket pagkatapos noon ay secondary market na — wala nang natatanggap ang banda kahit gaano kataas ang bagong presyo ng resale.
Field noteThis is what Section 74's buyback mechanics do in reverse — spending cash to shrink the float instead of raising cash by growing it.

Reading the Tape: Time & Sales

This notebook is named after this exact skill — watching the live stream of every executed trade, not just the chart it eventually draws.

The order book (Section 20) shows resting orders that haven't traded yet. Time & Sales — "the tape" — shows the opposite: every trade that has actually executed, in the order it happened, with price, size, and typically an aggressor side (whether the trade hit the bid or lifted the ask). A burst of large prints hitting the bid repeatedly is aggressive selling pressure; a string of prints lifting the ask is aggressive buying. A single unusually large print — a block trade — often reflects institutional size (Section 70) and can matter more than dozens of small retail-sized prints around it.

Explain it like I'm 5

If the order book is the menu of what people are willing to pay, the tape is the receipt printer at the register — showing every sale that actually happened, the second it happens.

Sa Tagalog: Kung ang order book ay parang menu ng gustong ibayad ng mga tao, ang tape ay parang printer ng resibo sa cashier — ipinapakita nito ang bawat totoong benta, sa mismong sandaling nangyari ito.
Field noteTraditional floor and early electronic traders literally read this scrolling ticker to gauge real-time conviction — a skill this whole notebook is named after, even though most modern traders mostly reconstruct the same read from Section 07's volume and Section 20's order book instead of the raw tape itself.

Stock Indices: How the S&P 500 and Dow Are Built

An index isn't a real, tradable thing on its own — it's a formula for averaging a basket of stocks, and which formula is used matters a lot.

A market-cap-weighted index (S&P 500, Nasdaq Composite) weights each company by its total market value, so a handful of the largest companies can move the whole index far more than most of the other members combined. A price-weighted index (the Dow Jones Industrial Average) instead simply averages its members' share prices — meaning a $500 stock moves the Dow more than a $50 stock, regardless of which company is actually bigger, a quirk unique to the Dow's formula. Index providers periodically rebalance membership, adding growing companies and removing shrinking ones; a stock's addition to a major index often causes a real, mechanical demand spike as index funds tracking that index are forced to buy it.

Explain it like I'm 5

Imagine grading a class two different ways: one method literally averages everyone's height (price-weighted), so one really tall kid skews it a lot. The other method weighs each kid by how much they actually weigh first (cap-weighted), so the biggest kid dominates instead — even if they're not the tallest.

Sa Tagalog: Isipin mo dalawang paraan ng pagsukat sa isang klase: literal na ina-average lang ang taas ng lahat (price-weighted), kaya isang mataas na bata lang ang sobrang nakakaapekto. Sa isa pa, tinitimbang muna ang bawat bata sa aktwal na bigat (cap-weighted), kaya yung pinakamalaki ang nangingibabaw — kahit hindi siya ang pinakamatangkad.
Field noteThis is Section 50's beta idea from the other direction — beta measures how a stock reacts to its index; this section explains why a handful of stocks can quietly be most of that index in the first place.

Dividends, Buybacks & Stock Splits

Section 46 mentioned these in passing. Here's the actual mechanics behind each one.

A dividend has three key dates: the declaration date (the board announces it), the ex-dividend date (buy on or after this date and you don't get the upcoming payment — the stock also typically opens lower by roughly the dividend amount that morning, the small predictable gap Section 46 mentioned), and the payment date (cash actually arrives). A stock split (say, 4-for-1) multiplies the share count and divides the price proportionally — a $400 stock becomes four $100 shares — changing nothing about the company's actual value, only making shares more accessible per unit. A reverse split (say, 1-for-10) does the opposite, often used by a struggling low-priced stock to avoid being delisted for trading under an exchange's minimum price. A buyback (Section 71) is an alternate way to return cash to shareholders, retiring shares instead of paying a dividend.

Explain it like I'm 5

A dividend is like a landlord mailing you a rent-share check on a set schedule. A stock split is like cutting one large pizza into more, smaller slices — same total pizza, just cut differently, so don't expect to eat more just because there are more slices.

Sa Tagalog: Ang dividend ay parang landlord na nagpapadala sa iyo ng tseke ng bahagi ng renta sa itinakdang iskedyul. Ang stock split ay parang paghihiwa ng isang malaking pizza sa mas maraming, mas maliliit na hiwa — pareho pa rin ang kabuuang pizza, iba lang ang paghihiwa, kaya huwag umasang mas marami kang makakain dahil lang mas marami ang hiwa.
Field noteNone of this changes what the company is actually worth — it only changes how that worth is packaged. Section 60's fundamentals, not the split ratio, decide whether the business itself is doing well.

Types of Stocks: Growth, Value & Market Cap

Not every stock is playing the same game — knowing which category you're actually holding changes what news should move it.

By size: large-cap (generally above roughly $10B, established companies), mid-cap, and small-cap (more volatile, less analyst coverage, more room to grow or fail). By style: growth stocks trade at higher valuations (Section 60's P/E ratio) on the expectation of fast future earnings growth, and are more sensitive to interest-rate changes (Section 53) since more of their value sits far in the future (tying to Section 76's bond-yield logic); value stocks trade cheaply relative to current earnings or assets, often in mature, slower-growing industries. Blue chip describes large, financially stable, long-track-record companies; penny stocks (typically under $5, often on smaller exchanges) carry high volatility, thin liquidity (Section 20), and a real risk of manipulation that fits neatly into several of Section 16's psychological traps.

Explain it like I'm 5

Growth stocks are like a promising rookie player everyone pays extra to have on the team, betting they'll be a superstar soon. Value stocks are like a proven veteran available cheap because they're no longer flashy, even though they're still reliable.

Sa Tagalog: Ang growth stocks ay parang promising na rookie na binabayaran ng lahat ng dagdag dahil naniniwalang magiging superstar siya balang araw. Ang value stocks ay parang beteranong player na mura kunin dahil hindi na kasing-flashy, kahit maaasahan pa rin.
Field noteA penny stock's chart can technically show every pattern in Sections 01–06 — the shapes still form, but thin liquidity means they're far easier to manipulate and far less trustworthy than the same shape on a large-cap chart.

Bonds & the Yield Curve

Stocks aren't the only thing moving on a rate decision. The bond market often moves first, and bigger.

A bond is a loan: the buyer lends money to a government or company in exchange for periodic interest (the coupon) and the return of principal at maturity. Bond prices and yields move inversely: when a bond's price falls, its fixed coupon represents a bigger percentage of the now-cheaper price, so yield rises — and vice versa. The yield curve plots yields across maturities; it normally slopes upward, since longer loans demand more yield for tying up money longer. An inverted yield curve — short-term yields sitting above long-term ones — has preceded most US recessions historically, a widely watched (if imperfect) macro signal. Since bonds compete with stocks for capital, rising yields (Section 53's rate decisions) generally pressure stock valuations, especially the growth stocks from Section 75.

Explain it like I'm 5

A bond is like putting money into a fixed-payment pact with a friend. When everyone suddenly wants that same pact, its resale price goes up and the effective deal for a new buyer goes down — that seesaw between price and yield is the whole mechanic.

Sa Tagalog: Ang bond ay parang paglalagay ng pera sa kasunduang may nakatakdang bayad mula sa kaibigan. Kapag biglang gusto ng lahat ng parehong kasunduan, tumataas ang resale price nito at bumababa ang epektibong deal para sa bagong bibili — iyan ang seesaw sa pagitan ng presyo at yield.
Field noteThis is the mechanism sitting underneath Section 53's rate decisions and Section 22's macro correlation — a rate move doesn't just affect BTC1! and stocks, it moves the entire bond market first, often within seconds of the announcement.

Regulators & Investor Protections

A different rulebook is watching every market in this notebook — worth knowing who, and what they actually do.

In the US, the SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission) regulates stock exchanges, public companies' disclosures, and enforces against fraud and insider trading; FINRA is a self-regulatory body overseeing brokerage firms and their registered representatives, and administers the PDT rule from Section 57. Forex (Section 47), being over-the-counter and global, has no single regulator at all — oversight depends on where your specific broker is licensed, part of why Section 47 flagged broker-dependent quotes as forex's own unique constraint.

How Currency Values Move: Fundamental Drivers

Section 53 covered interest rates moving currencies. Here's the rest of what actually sits underneath a currency's longer-run value.

Beyond interest-rate differentials (Section 47's carry trade), a country's trade balance matters: a trade surplus (exporting more than importing) creates natural foreign demand for the currency to pay for those exports, while a persistent deficit tends to weaken it over time. Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) is the long-run theory that identical goods should cost the same across countries once exchange rates are accounted for — real-world prices drift from this constantly, but large, sustained deviations are a slow-moving pull worth knowing exists. Capital flows — foreign investment chasing higher returns or safety — can dominate over trade in the short run, especially for reserve currencies. The US dollar, Japanese yen, and Swiss franc are classic safe haven currencies that tend to strengthen during broad risk-off periods (Section 22) regardless of that country's own individual data, simply because capital flees to perceived safety during stress.

Explain it like I'm 5

A country's currency is like a local sports team everyone wants a piece of when it's winning (trade surplus, high returns) — and like the one umbrella everyone runs for the moment it starts raining everywhere else (safe haven flows during a storm).

Sa Tagalog: Ang currency ng isang bansa ay parang lokal na sports team na gustong maging parte ng lahat kapag panalo ito (trade surplus, mataas na kita) — at parang iisang payong na sinasaklolo ng lahat sa oras na umulan sa lahat ng dako (safe haven flows habang may bagyo).
Field noteThese forces can point in different directions at once — a country can have a weak trade balance but still see its currency strengthen purely from safe-haven capital flows during a global risk-off move (Section 22). Read them together, not in isolation.

Chart Types: Line, Bar, Renko & Heikin-Ashi

Every diagram so far in this notebook has been a candlestick. It's not the only way to draw the same price data — each alternative throws away different information on purpose.

A line chart connects only closing prices — the simplest view, useful for a clean read of overall trend without wick noise. A bar chart (OHLC) packs the same four numbers as a candlestick (Section 01) into tick marks instead of a colored box — functionally identical information, just a different visual style, common on older platforms. Heikin-Ashi averages each candle's open/close with the prior candle's values before plotting, smoothing out noise so trends look cleaner and choppy periods show more small-bodied candles — the tradeoff is that Heikin-Ashi's open/close no longer match the instrument's real traded prices, so it's better for reading trend character than for placing an exact stop or entry (Section 35). Renko charts ignore time entirely, plotting a new brick only once price moves a fixed amount — filtering out small chop completely, at the cost of losing any sense of how long each move actually took.

Explain it like I'm 5

Think of the same short video played four ways: normal speed with all the shaky handheld footage (candlesticks/bars), a smoothed slow-motion cut with the camera shake removed (Heikin-Ashi), a plain summary sentence of where the scene started and ended (line chart), and a highlight reel that only cuts to a new clip once something big enough actually happens, ignoring how much real time passed between cuts (Renko).

Sa Tagalog: Isipin mo ang parehong maikling video, ipinalabas sa apat na paraan: normal na bilis na may kaunting yanig ng kamera (candlestick/bar), pinakinis na slow-motion na tinanggal ang yanig (Heikin-Ashi), simpleng pangungusap kung saan nagsimula at natapos ang eksena (line chart), at highlight reel na lilipat lang sa bagong clip kapag may sapat nang nangyari, hindi alintana kung gaano na katagal ang lumipas (Renko).
Field noteIf you switch to Heikin-Ashi or Renko for a cleaner trend read, switch back to a real candlestick or bar chart before placing an actual stop — Section 35's stop placement needs the instrument's real traded price, not a smoothed or time-warped substitute.

Options Greeks: Delta, Theta, Gamma & Vega

Section 43 covered what a call and put do. The Greeks are how traders actually measure how an option's price reacts to everything around it.

Delta measures how much an option's price moves per $1 move in the underlying — a call's delta runs 0 to 1 (a 0.50 delta call moves roughly $0.50 for every $1 the underlying moves), and it also roughly approximates the option's probability of expiring in the money. Theta is time decay — the value an option loses per day, all else equal, as expiration approaches; it accelerates in the final weeks before expiry, which is why an option buyer is fighting the clock even when direction is right. Gamma measures how fast Delta itself changes as the underlying moves — highest for at-the-money options near expiration, meaning a position's directional exposure can shift quickly. Vega measures sensitivity to implied volatility — a spike in expected volatility, around Section 23's news events for instance, can lift an option's price even before the underlying has actually moved.

Explain it like I'm 5

If an option's price is a car's speedometer, Delta is how fast you're going right now, Theta is fuel quietly burning off just by having the engine on, Gamma is how quickly your speed itself can change when you tap the gas, and Vega is how much rougher or smoother the road is expected to get.

Sa Tagalog: Kung ang presyo ng option ay parang speedometer ng kotse, ang Delta ay kung gaano kabilis ka ngayon, ang Theta ay gasolinang tahimik na nauubos kahit naka-on lang ang makina, ang Gamma ay kung gaano kabilis magbabago ang bilis mo kapag tinapik mo ang gasolina, at ang Vega ay kung gaano kagaspang o kadulas ang inaasahang kalsada.
Field noteAn option buyer needs the underlying to move enough, fast enough, to outrun Theta — a technically "correct" direction call can still lose money if it takes too long to play out.

Crypto Custody: Wallets & Exchange Risk

This notebook has spent 80 sections teaching you to read price. None of that matters if the coins themselves aren't actually safe.

Holding Bitcoin means controlling a private key — the cryptographic secret that proves ownership and authorizes spending; whoever holds the key controls the coins, regardless of any account name attached to it. A custodial exchange wallet means the exchange holds the private key on your behalf — convenient for active trading, but "not your keys, not your coins": if the exchange is hacked, mismanaged, or becomes insolvent, customer funds have been lost or frozen in real, well-documented past cases. A self-custody wallet keeps the private key under your own control, most securely in a hardware ("cold") wallet kept offline, or less securely in a software ("hot") wallet on an always-connected device. The common practical split: keep active trading capital on a reputable exchange, and move longer-term holdings to self-custody — treating exchange balances the way Section 62 treats open portfolio risk, not as a permanent parking spot.

Explain it like I'm 5

Keeping crypto on an exchange is like keeping cash in someone else's safe because it's convenient — fine, as long as that person and their safe are trustworthy and never robbed. Self-custody is like burying your own safe in the backyard — nobody else can lose it for you, but if you lose the map, nobody can help you dig it back up either.

Sa Tagalog: Ang paglalagay ng crypto sa exchange ay parang paglalagay ng pera sa safe ng iba dahil maginhawa — okay lang, hangga't mapagkakatiwalaan ang taong iyon at hindi nanakawan ang safe niya. Ang self-custody ay parang paglilibing ng sarili mong safe sa bakuran — walang ibang makakawala nito para sa iyo, pero kung nawala ang mapa, walang makakatulong sa iyong hukayin ito pabalik.
Field noteA perpetual futures position (Section 67) can only exist on an exchange — custody risk is unavoidable while it's open. That's one more real cost sitting quietly underneath a perp position, alongside the funding rate itself.

Stablecoins & Crypto Market Plumbing

Most of the crypto trading volume in this notebook's world doesn't actually flow through dollars directly — it flows through a dollar-pegged token instead.

A stablecoin is a crypto token designed to hold a steady value, usually pegged 1:1 to the US dollar — USDT (Tether) and USDC (Circle) are the two largest. Fiat-collateralized stablecoins hold reserves (cash and short-term instruments) meant to back every token in circulation; the issuer's own transparency and audit practices are the entire basis for trusting that peg holds. Stablecoins are the actual settlement currency behind most crypto trading and perpetual futures (Section 67) — moving value between exchanges in stablecoins is faster and cheaper than moving actual bank wires. A depeg — the stablecoin trading meaningfully away from $1 — is a real, if rare, event that has happened to major stablecoins during periods of acute stress, and is itself a systemic risk signal worth treating with the same seriousness as Section 81's custody risk.

Explain it like I'm 5

A stablecoin is like a casino chip that's supposed to always be worth exactly one dollar at the cashier window, backed by the casino actually keeping a dollar in the vault for every chip out there. If people ever doubt the vault really has that dollar for every chip, the chip's own trading price can wobble away from a dollar — even though nothing about the games themselves changed.

Sa Tagalog: Ang stablecoin ay parang chip sa casino na dapat palaging katumbas ng eksaktong isang dolyar sa cashier, dahil may isang dolyar na nakalagay sa vault para sa bawat chip na ipinamahagi. Kung nagduda ang mga tao na talagang kumpleto ang laman ng vault para sa bawat chip, maaaring gumalaw ang presyo ng chip palayo sa isang dolyar — kahit walang nagbago sa mismong mga laro.
Field noteThe funding rate in Section 67 is typically paid in stablecoins, not the underlying crypto itself — one more reason a perp trader is implicitly exposed to stablecoin and exchange risk, not just price risk.

On-Chain Metrics: Reading Bitcoin's Own Ledger

Section 60 covered reading a company's financials. Bitcoin has no company — but its public blockchain is its own kind of financial statement, readable by anyone.

Because every Bitcoin transaction is recorded on a public ledger, analysts build indicators directly from that data instead of price alone. MVRV (Market Value to Realized Value) compares BTC's current market cap to the aggregate price paid by everyone still holding their coins — a high reading suggests the average holder is sitting on large unrealized profit (historically associated with market tops), a low or negative reading suggests widespread unrealized loss (historically associated with capitulation, tying to Section 09's Wyckoff selling climax). SOPR (Spent Output Profit Ratio) looks at coins actually moving on-chain right now: a reading above 1 means coins moving today are, on average, being sold at a profit; below 1 means at a loss. Exchange netflow tracks coins moving onto exchanges (often preceding selling) versus off exchanges into self-custody (Section 81), often read as accumulation intent. None of these replace the price chart — they're a second, independent lens Section 30's confluence check can weigh alongside it.

Explain it like I'm 5

If price is what something is selling for right now, on-chain data is like being able to see the actual receipt every single past buyer got, for free. MVRV is basically asking: on average, is everyone still holding sitting on a big profit or a big loss right now — and history shows the mood at those two extremes tends to matter.

Sa Tagalog: Kung presyo ang kasalukuyang binebentahan ng isang bagay, ang on-chain data ay parang nakikita mo ang aktwal na resibo ng bawat nakaraang bumili, nang libre. Ang MVRV ay parang tinatanong: sa average, malaki bang tubo o lugi ang hawak ngayon ng lahat ng may-ari — at ipinapakita ng kasaysayan na mahalaga ang mood sa dalawang sukdulang ito.
Field noteOn-chain metrics are Bitcoin-specific by nature — stocks and forex have no equivalent public ledger, which is exactly why Section 60's traditional fundamentals exist for them instead.

Monetary Policy Tools: QE, QT & the Money Supply

Section 53 covered a central bank moving one number: the interest rate. Here's the rest of the toolkit that moved markets even harder in 2020–2022.

Beyond the policy rate (Section 53), central banks influence markets through the money supply itself. Quantitative Easing (QE) is a central bank buying large quantities of government bonds and other assets, injecting new money into the financial system, pushing bond yields down (Section 76) and pushing investors toward riskier assets — a major driver behind stocks' and BTC's 2020–2021 rally. Quantitative Tightening (QT) reverses this: the central bank lets those holdings run off or actively sells them, withdrawing liquidity from the system, generally a headwind for risk assets. The reserve requirement (how much of deposits banks must hold rather than lend out) is a third, less frequently used lever. A central bank's own balance sheet size is itself a widely watched chart, since a shrinking balance sheet has historically coincided with tighter financial conditions across stocks, bonds, and crypto alike.

Explain it like I'm 5

QE is like a landlord flooding the neighborhood with easy, cheap loans so everyone has spare cash to spend and invest, pushing prices of nearly everything up. QT is the landlord calling those loans back in, pulling spare cash back out of the system, and things generally cool off.

Sa Tagalog: Ang QE ay parang landlord na binabahaan ng madali at murang pautang ang buong kapitbahayan, para may sobrang pera ang lahat na gagastusin at iinbestiyahan, kaya tumataas ang presyo ng halos lahat. Ang QT ay ang landlord na binabawi ang mga pautang, hinahatak palabas ang sobrang pera, at karaniwang lumalamig ang lahat.
Field noteThis is the mechanism underneath Section 22's risk-on/risk-off swings on a multi-year scale — QE and QT are slow tides, not daily currents, but they set the water level everything else is swimming in.

Trading Costs: Commissions, Fees & Rollover

Section 56 covered slippage. This is the other half of trading cost — the fees you'd pay even in a perfectly liquid market.

A commission is a flat or per-unit fee a broker charges to execute a trade; many modern stock brokers advertise "zero commission" but often earn revenue elsewhere (routing order flow, spread markup) instead. Futures and forex brokers typically charge per-contract or per-lot commissions plus the natural bid-ask spread (Section 20). Holding a leveraged position overnight can trigger a swap or rollover fee (forex) or the funding rate (Section 67, for crypto perpetuals) — a real, recurring cost distinct from any price movement. None of these show up as a dramatic single loss, but they compound: a strategy with a real edge (Section 21's expectancy) can still bleed out if trading costs eat a meaningful share of the average win.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like a job that pays well on paper but has a toll booth, a parking fee, and a small daily equipment rental you barely notice — each one small, but add them up over a year and a meaningful chunk of the paycheck is gone before you even see it.

Sa Tagalog: Parang trabahong maganda ang sahod sa papel pero may toll, bayad sa paradahan, at maliit na arawang upa sa kagamitan na hindi mo halos napapansin — maliit man isa-isa, kung pagsasama-samahin sa loob ng isang taon, malaking bahagi na ng sahod ang nawala bago mo pa ito makita.
Field noteAdd expected trading costs into your Section 25 trading plan and Section 44 backtests directly, as a per-trade line item — not as an afterthought once real results start disappointing you.

Backtesting Pitfalls: Overfitting & Survivorship Bias

Section 44 showed you how to backtest a rule. This is how a backtest quietly lies to you even when you followed every step correctly.

Overfitting (or "curve-fitting") happens when a rule set gets tuned with so many specific parameters that it perfectly explains the past data it was built on, but has really just memorized that data's noise rather than a real, repeatable edge — a warning sign is a strategy that needs many finely-tuned conditions to work at all. Survivorship bias creeps in when a backtest only includes assets or companies that still exist today, silently excluding everything that went bankrupt or got delisted along the way — inflating historical performance because the failures are invisible in the dataset. The fix for both: test on out-of-sample data the rule wasn't tuned on, keep rule sets as simple as the edge honestly requires, and specifically seek out a dataset that includes the failures, not just the survivors.

Explain it like I'm 5

Overfitting is like memorizing the exact answers to last year's specific test instead of actually learning the subject — you'll ace a test with those exact questions again, and completely fall apart on this year's different but related one. Survivorship bias is like judging how safe a hiking trail is only by interviewing hikers who successfully finished it, never the ones who had to turn back.

Sa Tagalog: Ang overfitting ay parang pagme-memorize ng eksaktong sagot sa huling taong test imbes na talagang pag-aralan ang paksa — panalo ka kung parehong tanong ulit, pero bagsak agad sa magkaibang tanong ngayong taon. Ang survivorship bias ay parang paghusga sa kaligtasan ng isang daanan sa bundok batay lang sa panayam sa mga nakatapos nito, hindi kailanman sa mga napilitang bumalik.
Field noteThis is Section 16's confirmation bias wearing a spreadsheet instead of a chart — the fix is the same instinct: actively look for the version of the test that could prove your rule wrong, not just the one that confirms it.

The Business Cycle

Bonds, central banks, and risk sentiment from earlier sections aren't separate stories — they're different views of the same recurring economic cycle.

The business cycle moves through four broad phases. Expansion: growth accelerates, employment rises, central banks (Section 53) often hold or raise rates to keep inflation in check. Peak: growth tops out, inflation pressure is typically highest, and the yield curve (Section 76) is more likely to flatten or invert. Contraction (recession): growth slows or reverses, unemployment rises, central banks typically cut rates and may resort to QE (Section 84) to support the economy. Trough: the cycle bottoms and a new expansion begins. Risk assets like stocks and BTC (Section 22's risk-on/risk-off) tend to struggle late in expansion and through contraction, while bonds often rally as rates fall heading into a slowdown. No two cycles play out identically, and calling the exact turning point in real time is notoriously difficult — this is a framework for context, the same caution Section 37 gave the halving cycle.

Explain it like I'm 5

The economy moves in a slow, recurring weather pattern: a sunny stretch that eventually gets too hot (expansion into peak), a storm that cools things down (contraction), and then clearing skies again (trough into the next expansion). Nobody can perfectly predict the exact day the weather turns, but knowing which season you're roughly in still changes what you should probably be wearing.

Sa Tagalog: Ang ekonomiya ay parang mabagal, umuulit na weather pattern: maaraw na yugto na eventually sumosobra ang init (expansion papuntang peak), bagyo na nagpapalamig (contraction), tapos luminaw ulit ang langit (trough papunta sa susunod na expansion). Walang perpektong makakahula ng eksaktong araw ng pagbabago ng panahon, pero ang malaman kung anong season ka humigit-kumulang ay nagbabago pa rin sa dapat mong isuot.
Field noteThis is the framework tying together Sections 53, 76, and 22 — rate decisions, the yield curve, and risk-on/risk-off sentiment are all symptoms of wherever the cycle currently sits, not independent stories.

Portfolio Construction & Asset Allocation

Every section before this one was about one trade at a time. Zoom out far enough, and the real question becomes how your money is split across everything at once.

Asset allocation is the decision of how to divide capital across broad categories — stocks, bonds (Section 76), cash, and increasingly crypto — before ever picking an individual position. The classic logic: assets that don't move in lockstep smooth out the ride, because when one category falls, another often holds steady or rises to offset it (the same uncorrelated-bet idea Section 54 raised for currency pairs, just applied across asset classes instead of within one). A traditional starting template — the "60/40" portfolio, 60% stocks and 40% bonds — leans on stocks for growth and bonds for ballast, since bonds have historically (not always — see 2022) held up or gained when stocks sold off hard.

Diversification only works if the pieces are genuinely different bets. Owning ten stocks that are all large-cap US tech (Section 75) is concentrated risk wearing a diversified costume — the same trap Section 54 warned about with correlated currency pairs. Rebalancing — periodically selling what's grown to restore the original target percentages and buying what's lagged — is the unglamorous discipline that actually captures "buy low, sell high" instead of just describing it after the fact. None of this replaces the single-trade risk management in Section 17; it's the layer above it, answering "how much of my total capital is even eligible to be risked this way" before any individual position sizing begins.

Explain it like I'm 5

Think of a fruit basket you set up to be half apples, half oranges. If apples have a great season and multiply, your basket quietly becomes mostly apples — tastier for apple fans, but not the balanced basket you originally wanted. Rebalancing is just trading some apples back for oranges so the basket looks like your original plan again, not a reaction to news or a guess about what happens next.

Sa Tagalog: Isipin mo ang basket ng prutas na ginawa mong kalahati mansanas, kalahati kahel. Kung maganda ang season ng mansanas at dumami ito, tahimik na nagiging mostly mansanas ang basket mo — masarap para sa fan ng mansanas, pero hindi na ang balanced na basket na orihinal mong gusto. Ang rebalancing ay pagpapalit lang ng ilang mansanas pabalik sa kahel para bumalik sa itsura ng orihinal mong plano — hindi ito reaksyon sa balita o hula sa susunod na mangyayari.
Pro TipSet a calendar rule, not a feeling rule — rebalance quarterly or annually, or only when an allocation drifts more than a set threshold (say, 5 percentage points) from target. Rebalancing "whenever it feels right" quietly turns into market timing wearing a diversification costume.
Field noteThis sits directly above Section 17's single-trade risk math and Section 62's portfolio heat — allocation decides how much capital is even in play across each asset class, before either of those finer-grained tools take over.

Understanding Inflation & Real vs Nominal Returns

Section 23 mentioned CPI as a scheduled news event. This is what that number actually measures — and why a "winning" trade can still quietly lose.

Inflation is the rate at which the general price level rises, eroding how much a fixed amount of currency can actually buy over time. CPI (Consumer Price Index) tracks this by pricing a fixed "basket" of everyday goods and services and measuring how that basket's total cost changes month to month; "core CPI" strips out the more volatile food and energy components to reveal the underlying trend. This connects directly to Section 53's rate decisions and Section 84's monetary policy: a central bank's primary tool for fighting high inflation is raising rates to cool demand, and its tool for fighting the opposite problem (deflation, or dangerously low inflation) is cutting rates or resorting to QE.

The distinction that actually matters for a trader or investor is nominal versus real return. A nominal return is the raw percentage gain before adjusting for anything; a real return subtracts inflation from that nominal number to show what was actually gained in purchasing power. A 5% nominal gain during a year of 6% inflation is a real loss of roughly 1% — the account balance grew, but it now buys less than it did a year ago. This is exactly why "cash" is not actually risk-free sitting in a bank earning near-zero interest during a high-inflation stretch — it's guaranteed to lose real value, just slowly and invisibly enough that most people never notice the erosion happening.

Explain it like I'm 5

Imagine your allowance goes up 5% this year, which sounds great — until you find out your favorite snack's price went up 6%. You technically have more pesos than before, but you can now buy fewer snacks than you could last year. That gap between "more money" and "more stuff that money can actually buy" is exactly what inflation eats.

Sa Tagalog: Isipin mong tumaas ng 5% ang baon mo ngayong taon, maganda pakinggan — hanggang malaman mong tumaas din ng 6% ang presyo ng paborito mong meryenda. Teknikal na mas marami kang piso kaysa dati, pero mas kaunti na ang meryendang kaya mong bilhin kumpara noong isang taon. Ang agwat na iyon sa pagitan ng "mas maraming pera" at "mas maraming bagay na kaya niyang bilhin" ang mismong kinakain ng inflation.
Pro TipAlways ask "nominal or real?" before comparing any return across different years — a 10% return in a 2%-inflation year and a 10% return in an 8%-inflation year are not the same win, even though the headline number looks identical.
Field noteThis is the exact mechanism behind Section 23's CPI release and Section 53's rate decisions — both exist because inflation, left unchecked in either direction, quietly breaks the value of the currency every price on your chart is denominated in.

Forex Margin & Leverage: Why It's Different

Section 18 covered leverage on CME futures. Retail forex brokers routinely offer leverage ratios futures traders would find shocking — and the regulatory reasons why differ by country.

Forex brokers commonly advertise leverage far beyond what CME futures margin (Section 18) allows — ratios like 50:1, 100:1, or even 500:1 at some offshore brokers, meaning a small deposit controls a position dozens or hundreds of times larger. This isn't a loophole exactly; forex majors (Section 47) genuinely move in smaller daily percentage swings than a volatile stock or crypto, which is part of the industry's justification for higher leverage limits. The math from Section 18 still applies exactly the same way, just with a much bigger multiplier: at 100:1 leverage, a mere 1% adverse move against the position wipes out the entire margin deposit.

Regulation varies sharply by jurisdiction, tying directly to Section 77's regulator lesson. US-regulated forex brokers, overseen by the CFTC and NFA, are capped at 50:1 leverage on major pairs by law — among the most conservative limits globally. Offshore brokers registered in more permissive jurisdictions can and do offer far higher leverage, which is exactly why Section 77 flagged "broker-dependent" as forex's own unique constraint: the same currency pair can carry wildly different real risk depending purely on which broker, and which country's rules, the account sits under.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like three amusement park rides labeled by how easily they tip over: a sturdy go-kart that needs a huge shove to flip (lower leverage), a wobbly bike that tips with a moderate push (moderate leverage), and a unicycle balanced on a tightrope that tips if you so much as sneeze (extreme offshore leverage). Same rider, same skill — wildly different odds of a bad ending just from which ride they picked.

Sa Tagalog: Parang tatlong magkaibang sakayan sa amusement park, base sa kadalian nilang matumba: matibay na go-kart na kailangan ng malakas na tulak bago matumba (mababang leverage), gumagalaw na bisikleta na tumatamba sa katamtamang tulak (katamtamang leverage), at unicycle sa lubid na tumatamba kahit bahagyang pagbahing (sobrang taas na offshore leverage). Pareho ang sumasakay, pareho ang galing — pero magkaiba nang husto ang tsansang mabigo, depende lang sa napiling sakayan.
Pro TipCheck your broker's actual regulator, not just its marketing page — a US-regulated account capped at 50:1 and an offshore account offering 500:1 are not the same product wearing different labels. They carry fundamentally different real risk and, per Section 77, different investor protections if something goes wrong.
Field noteWhatever leverage is available, Section 17's 1% rule still decides how much to actually risk — higher leverage just means a smaller position controls the same dollar risk, exactly like Section 18 already taught for futures.

Reading an Earnings Report: Beats, Misses & Guidance

Section 46 flagged earnings as a stock's biggest scheduled volatility event. This is what actually moves the price when the report drops.

The headline number is EPS (earnings per share) — net profit divided by shares outstanding — compared against the analyst consensus estimate. A "beat" means actual EPS came in above that estimate, a "miss" means below; revenue gets the same beat/miss treatment against its own consensus. The counterintuitive part every trader eventually learns: a genuine beat can still send the stock falling, and a miss can send it rallying — because the market was already pricing in expectations before the report, and the reaction is to the surprise relative to those expectations, not the raw numbers in isolation.

Guidance — management's own forward-looking estimate for the next quarter or year — often moves the stock more than the earnings that already happened, precisely because Section 60's fundamentals-based investing cares more about where the business is headed than where it's already been. Weak guidance following a strong beat is a common pattern that confuses beginners: the past quarter genuinely was good, but the market is a forward-pricing mechanism, and it's already trading on the next one. Earnings calls (where management takes analyst questions live) frequently move the stock further than the numbers alone, especially around tone on guidance, margins, and any unexpected disclosure.

Explain it like I'm 5

Imagine getting a report card with a solid B+, which sounds fine — but your parents were expecting an A after you'd been bragging all month, so they're disappointed anyway. Now imagine a C+ that's actually better than the D they were bracing for, and they're thrilled. The grade itself matters less than how it compares to what everyone already expected — and even more than the report card itself, what worries or excites people is what you tell them about your plan for next semester.

Sa Tagalog: Isipin mo tumanggap ka ng report card na B+, maganda naman — pero umaasa ang magulang mo ng A dahil ipinagmamalaki mo buong buwan, kaya nabigo pa rin sila. Ngayon isipin mo ang C+ na mas maganda pala kaysa sa D na inaasahan nila, at natuwa sila. Mas mahalaga kung paano ito ikinumpara sa inaasahan kumpara sa grado mismo — at mas mahalaga pa, kung ano ang sinabi mo tungkol sa plano mo sa susunod na semester.
Pro TipCheck the consensus estimate before the report, not after — knowing what the market already expects is the only way to judge whether a "beat" was actually a surprise, or just a number everyone already saw coming and had already priced in.
Field noteThis is Section 23's news-event volatility, scheduled quarterly per company instead of macro-wide — the same "know the calendar, know your position" discipline applies either way.

Reading a Balance Sheet: Assets, Liabilities & Equity 101

Section 60 mentioned debt levels as a fundamental to check. This is the actual document that number comes from, and how to read it in under a minute.

A balance sheet is a snapshot, at one specific date, of everything a company owns (assets), everything it owes (liabilities), and what's left over for shareholders (equity) — captured in the fundamental identity Assets = Liabilities + Equity, which always balances by definition, hence the name. Assets split into current (cash, inventory, anything convertible to cash within a year) and long-term (property, equipment, intangibles like patents). Liabilities split the same way: current (bills due within a year, short-term debt) and long-term (bonds issued, long-term loans — the same kind of instrument Section 76 covered from the buyer's side, now seen from the issuing company's side of the ledger).

A handful of ratios turn these raw numbers into something comparable across companies. The current ratio (current assets ÷ current liabilities) checks short-term solvency — comfortably above 1 means the company can cover its near-term bills without scrambling. The debt-to-equity ratio (total liabilities ÷ shareholder equity) measures how much of the company is funded by debt versus owner capital — a high ratio means more fragility in a downturn, tying directly to Section 60's original debt-level warning and Section 76's point that rising rates make existing and new debt more expensive to service. None of this predicts tomorrow's candle any more than the P/E ratio does — it's the other half of Section 60's fundamentals-decide-the-watchlist, technicals-decide-the-entry framework.

Explain it like I'm 5

A balance sheet is like a snapshot of your own finances on one specific day: everything you own (your phone, your savings, your bike) equals everything you owe (a loan from a friend, a credit card bill) plus whatever's genuinely yours free and clear. Owning a lot of stuff sounds great until you check how much of it is actually still owed to someone else.

Sa Tagalog: Ang balance sheet ay parang litrato ng sarili mong pananalapi sa isang partikular na araw: lahat ng pag-aari mo (telepono, ipon, bisikleta) ay katumbas ng lahat ng utang mo (utang sa kaibigan, bill sa credit card) kasama ang talagang sa iyo lang, walang utang. Ang pagmamay-ari ng maraming bagay ay maganda pakinggan hanggang tingnan mo kung gaano karami dito ang utang pa pala sa iba.
Pro TipDon't just check whether a company has debt — check the trend across several quarters. Debt that's steadily shrinking while revenue grows tells a very different story than debt that's quietly climbing every quarter, even if both companies show the exact same debt-to-equity number today.
Field noteThis is where Section 60's "debt levels" line actually comes from — and why Section 76's rate environment matters here too: the same debt load gets more expensive to service, and more dangerous, when yields rise.

Bitcoin Dominance & Altcoin Season

This notebook has focused almost entirely on BTC1! itself. Zoom out to the whole crypto market, and Bitcoin's own share of it tells a story worth knowing.

Bitcoin Dominance (often shown as "BTC.D") is Bitcoin's market cap as a percentage of the total crypto market cap — when it's high, Bitcoin is capturing most of the capital and attention; when it's falling, capital is rotating into altcoins (every cryptocurrency that isn't Bitcoin) instead. A rising BTC.D during a broad crypto rally often signals a "flight to relative quality" within crypto itself — Bitcoin acting as the sector's large-cap blue chip from Section 75's framework, while smaller, more speculative altcoins lag behind.

"Altcoin season" describes the opposite phase: BTC.D falling while altcoins collectively outperform Bitcoin, often after Bitcoin has already made a large move and capital searches for the next opportunity further out on the risk curve — the same risk-seeking behavior Section 68's sentiment extremes and Section 16's psychology chapter both warn tends to peak with excess, not clear thinking. Altcoins are typically far more volatile and far less liquid (Section 20) than Bitcoin itself, meaning the same position-sizing discipline from Section 17 matters even more there, not less, however exciting the rotation looks in the moment.

Explain it like I'm 5

Bitcoin dominance is like checking what share of total cafeteria spending goes to the most popular, most trusted food stall versus every smaller, newer stall combined. When everyone's nervous, they crowd back to the trusted stall. When everyone's feeling adventurous, they scatter out to try the newer, riskier stalls instead — and those smaller stalls can run out of food (liquidity) a lot faster than the big one.

Sa Tagalog: Ang Bitcoin dominance ay parang pagtingin kung gaano kalaki ang share ng kabuuang gastos sa cafeteria ng paaralan na napupunta sa pinaka-sikat at pinagkakatiwalaang tindahan, kumpara sa lahat ng mas maliliit at bagong tindahan. Kapag kinakabahan ang lahat, bumabalik sila sa pinagkakatiwalaang tindahan. Kapag masaya at ganahang sumubok ang lahat, kumakalat sila sa mas maliliit at riskier na tindahan — at mas mabilis maubusan ng stock (liquidity) ang mga maliliit na tindahan kaysa sa malaki.
Pro TipWatch BTC.D as sector-level context, the same way Section 22 watches DXY and VIX for the broader market — it won't tell you which specific altcoin to buy, but a clear BTC.D trend tells you which direction the crypto sector's capital is currently rotating.
Field noteAn altcoin's chart still obeys every tool from Section 01 onward — but with thinner liquidity (Section 20) behind it, the same pattern is inherently less reliable than the identical shape on BTC1! itself.

Compound Interest & the Rule of 72

Every calculation in this notebook so far has been about one trade. This is the quiet math working in the background over years, for better or worse.

Compound interest is interest earned on interest already earned — not just on the original amount. A return that compounds annually grows faster each year because the base it's calculated on keeps growing too; the classic illustration is that $1,000 growing at 10% annually becomes roughly $1,100 after year one, but by year ten it's not $2,000 (simple 10%-a-year addition) — it's roughly $2,594, because each year's 10% was calculated on an already-bigger number. Time in the market, not just the return rate, is the multiplier that makes compounding powerful: the same $1,000 at 10% for 30 years grows past $17,000, nearly seven times the 10-year result, from twenty extra years alone.

The Rule of 72 is a quick mental-math shortcut: divide 72 by the annual return percentage to estimate how many years it takes to double. At 8% annual return, money roughly doubles every 9 years (72÷8); at 12%, every 6 years. This cuts both ways — the same math applied to Section 89's inflation rate estimates how fast purchasing power halves instead of how fast money doubles: at 6% inflation, the real value of uninvested cash roughly halves every 12 years (72÷6). Compounding is also exactly why Section 86's trading costs and Section 21's expectancy matter more over a trading career than any single quarter suggests — a small, steady edge (or a small, steady leak) compounds into something very large given enough time.

Explain it like I'm 5

It's like a snowball rolling down a snowy hill. At the very top, it grows slowly because it's small. But as it grows bigger, it picks up even more snow with every single roll, so the growth speeds up on its own — not because the hill got steeper, but because the snowball itself got bigger.

Sa Tagalog: Parang bola ng niyebe na gumugulong pababa ng maniyebeng burol. Sa umpisa, mabagal itong lumaki dahil maliit pa. Pero habang lumalaki, mas maraming niyebe ang nadadala nito sa bawat ikot, kaya bumibilis ang paglaki nang mag-isa — hindi dahil tumarik ang burol, kundi dahil lumaki na mismo ang bola.
Pro TipUse the Rule of 72 as a gut-check on any advertised return, in either direction: an investment promising to "10x in 2 years" implies roughly 72÷2 = 36% needed just to double once, let alone ten times over — a useful, instant reality check before believing a pitch.
Field noteThis is the multi-year version of Section 86's trading costs and Section 21's expectancy — a tiny, steady edge (or a tiny, steady leak) looks negligible on any single trade and enormous once compounded across a whole career.

Tax-Loss Harvesting

Section 38 covered how futures gains get taxed. This is a deliberate, legal technique for managing the loss side of that same tax bill.

Tax-loss harvesting means deliberately selling a losing position to realize (lock in) the loss for tax purposes, which can then offset realized gains elsewhere in the portfolio, reducing the total tax owed for the year — a technique available to spot crypto and stock holdings taxed on realized gains, distinct from Section 38's mark-to-market futures treatment, which taxes gains whether or not a position was actually closed. In the US, realized losses first offset realized gains of the same type (short-term against short-term, long-term against long-term), and a limited amount of leftover net loss (currently $3,000 per year for individuals) can offset ordinary income, with any excess carried forward to future tax years.

The wash-sale rule is the important guardrail: selling a security at a loss and buying back a substantially identical one within 30 days (before or after) disallows the loss for tax purposes in the US — meaning a trader can't sell for a tax write-off in the morning and simply rebuy the identical position in the afternoon to stay in the trade. Crypto has historically sat in a gray area on wash-sale rules in various jurisdictions (rules written for securities not always clearly covering crypto), but relying on a technicality lasting forever is exactly the kind of thing Section 38's field note already warned about: confirm current rules with a tax professional before relying on any of this, since tax law changes and genuinely varies by country.

Explain it like I'm 5

Imagine you won 5,000 pesos at one game booth but lost 2,000 at another, both on the same day at the same fair. Tax-loss harvesting is like being allowed to show the fair's cashier both tickets together, so you only get taxed on the net 3,000 pesos you actually walked away with — not the full 5,000 you won at just one booth.

Sa Tagalog: Isipin mong nanalo ka ng 5,000 piso sa isang laro pero natalo ka ng 2,000 sa isa pa, parehong araw, parehong perya. Ang tax-loss harvesting ay parang pinapayagan kang ipakita ang dalawang resibo nang magkasama sa cashier ng perya, para ang 3,000 pisong netong dala mo pauwi lang ang masisingil ng buwis — hindi yung buong 5,000 na nanalo ka sa isang booth lang.
Pro TipHarvest losses with a plan, not panic — the point is realizing a loss you'd have taken anyway per your own exit rules (Section 36), timed to also help at tax time, not selling a position you actually still believe in purely to generate a write-off.
Field noteThis only applies to positions actually taxed on realized gains — Section 38's Section 1256 futures contracts are marked to market and taxed annually regardless of whether you closed them, so harvesting in the traditional sense doesn't apply there the same way.

Glossary

Quick reference for terms used above.

OHLC
Open, High, Low, Close — the four prices that define one candle.
BOS (Break of Structure)
Price takes out the prior swing high/low in the direction of the trend, confirming continuation.
CHoCH (Change of Character)
The first swing that breaks the established HH/HL or LH/LL sequence — an early warning of possible reversal.
Liquidity
Clusters of resting stop-loss and pending orders, often just beyond an obvious swing high/low — a common magnet for price.
Spring
A brief false breakdown below a trading range that quickly reclaims the range, trapping short sellers before markup.
Upthrust
The distribution mirror of a spring — a false breakout above a range that fails and reverses down.
CME Gap
An empty space on the CME Bitcoin futures chart where price jumped between Friday's close and the next session's open because spot moved while futures were closed.
Role Reversal
A broken resistance zone acting as new support on retest, or vice versa.
R:R (Risk:Reward)
Distance from entry to target, divided by distance from entry to stop — how much you stand to make versus lose.
MA20 / MA200
Average closing price over the last 20 or 200 candles. MA20 tracks short-term trend; MA200 marks the broader bullish/bearish regime.
Golden Cross / Death Cross
A faster MA crossing above (golden) or below (death) a slower MA — classically the 50-day over the 200-day.
Bollinger Bands
A moving average (basis) plus an upper and lower band drawn two standard deviations away, widening with volatility and squeezing when it dries up.
Head & Shoulders
Three swing highs, the middle one tallest, sitting on a "neckline" — a classic reversal pattern. Upside-down, it's an Inverse Head & Shoulders (bullish).
Double Top / Bottom
Two failed attempts at the same high (top, bearish) or same low (bottom, bullish) — shaped like an M or a W.
Flag / Pennant
A brief, tight sideways or counter-trend pause after a strong move, usually resolving in the direction of that original move.
Marubozu
A candle with no wicks at all, or almost none — one side had full control from open to close, no rejection either way.
Harami
A small candle sitting entirely inside the prior candle's body — the mirror-image cousin of an engulfing pattern, showing momentum stalling rather than reversing outright.
Three White Soldiers / Three Black Crows
Three consecutive strong candles in the same direction with little overlap — a forceful, sustained push by one side.
Fibonacci Retracement
Horizontal levels (23.6%, 38.2%, 50%, 61.8%, 78.6%) dividing a price swing, marking where a pullback often stalls before the trend resumes.
RSI (Relative Strength Index)
A 0–100 oscillator comparing recent up-moves to down-moves; above 70 is "overbought," below 30 "oversold."
MACD
The gap between a fast and slow moving average, plotted against its own signal line — crossovers flag a shift in momentum.
Divergence
Price makes a new high/low while an oscillator (RSI/MACD) doesn't confirm it — a warning that the move's underlying momentum is weakening.
Market / Limit / Stop Order
Market fills immediately at the current price; Limit only fills at a chosen price or better; Stop becomes a market order once a trigger price is hit.
Contract Rollover
The point where a continuous futures symbol (like BTC1!) switches its data feed from an expiring contract to the next quarterly one.
FOMO
Fear Of Missing Out — entering a trade late, without a plan, purely because the move already looks like it's happening without you.
Margin / Leverage
Margin is the collateral you put up; leverage is the multiple of exposure that collateral controls. Both gains and losses scale with the multiple.
Margin Call / Liquidation
A demand for more collateral when a leveraged position's equity falls too low; liquidation is the position being force-closed when it isn't met.
VWAP
Volume-Weighted Average Price — the session's average traded price, weighted by volume at each level; resets every session.
Bid / Ask / Spread
The bid is the highest resting buy order, the ask the lowest resting sell order; the spread is the gap between them.
Expectancy
(Win% × Avg Win) − (Loss% × Avg Loss) — the actual measure of whether a strategy is profitable over many trades, not win rate alone.
Risk-On / Risk-Off
Market-wide moods where money flows into riskier assets (risk-on) or out of them into safety (risk-off), typically moving stocks, BTC, and the US dollar together or in opposition.
Elliott Wave
A theory that trends unfold in a five-wave impulse (with the trend) followed by a three-wave correction (against it), repeating at different scales.
Harmonic Pattern
A precise XABCD price structure where each leg matches a specific Fibonacci ratio, pointing to a Potential Reversal Zone (PRZ).
Trading Session
One of the three broad global trading windows — Asian, London, New York — whose overlaps typically bring the highest volume and volatility.
Confluence
Multiple independent tools (a zone, a fib level, an average, a volume signature) all pointing to the same price area at once — the basis for a higher-conviction read.
Ichimoku Cloud (Kumo)
A five-line indicator whose Senkou Span A/B lines are plotted 26 periods ahead, shading a projected support/resistance zone; price above it is bullish, below bearish, inside it unresolved.
Tenkan-sen / Kijun-sen
Ichimoku's fast (9-period) and slow (26-period) midpoint lines — their crossover behaves like the MA golden/death cross from Section 10.
ATR (Average True Range)
A moving average of True Range (the largest of a candle's high-low, high-prev.close, or low-prev.close distance) — a measure of how much an instrument is actually moving, used to size stops to current volatility.
Pivot Points
Support/resistance levels (P, R1–R3, S1–S3) computed from the previous session's high, low, and close using a fixed formula — identical on every platform, no drawing required.
Trailing Stop
A stop-loss that moves up (in a long) behind price as a trend extends, locking in more profit while leaving room for the trend to continue.
Scaling Out
Closing part of a position at a first target while letting the remainder ride toward a further one, often with a trailing stop.
Halving
A scheduled event roughly every four years that cuts Bitcoin's new-issuance rate in half — historically associated with (not a guarantee of) major long-term price cycles.
Section 1256 Contract
A US tax category covering regulated futures like CME Bitcoin futures — gains/losses get blanket 60% long-term / 40% short-term treatment and are marked to market at year-end, regardless of actual holding period.
Open Interest
The total number of outstanding (not yet closed) futures contracts — a running balance, unlike volume, that rises with fresh positioning and falls as positions close.
Contango / Backwardation
Contango is futures trading above spot price; backwardation is futures trading below it. The gap (the basis) shrinks to zero as a contract nears expiration.
Price Limit / Circuit Breaker
An exchange-mandated pause or range restriction triggered when price moves too far too fast — a mechanic CME futures have that unregulated 24/7 spot crypto exchanges don't.
Call / Put Option
A call is the right (not obligation) to buy at a set strike price before expiration; a put is the right to sell. Buying a put against a long position is a classic hedge.
Market Maker
A firm that continuously quotes both a bid and an ask, profiting from the spread rather than betting on direction, and providing the liquidity retail orders trade against.
PDT Rule (Pattern Day Trader)
A US rule restricting margin stock accounts under $25,000 to three day-trades per rolling five-business-day period — unique to stocks, no equivalent in futures or forex.
Pip / Lot
A pip is forex's standard smallest price increment (usually the 4th decimal); a lot is a position-size unit — standard (100,000), mini (10,000), or micro (1,000) units of the base currency.
Base / Quote Currency
In a pair like EUR/USD, the base (EUR) is what's being bought or sold; the quote (USD) is what it's priced in.
Carry Trade
Holding a high-interest-rate currency funded by borrowing a low-interest-rate one, earning the rate differential as long as the exchange rate doesn't move against it enough to erase the gain.
Earnings Report
A public company's quarterly results release — often a stock's single biggest scheduled volatility event, the equivalent of Section 23's news calendar but specific to one stock.
Short Squeeze
A sharp rally in a heavily-shorted stock that forces short sellers to buy back shares to cover, adding fuel to the very rally hurting them.
Borrow Fee / Hard to Borrow
The cost (or unavailability) of the shares a broker must lend you to open a short position — popular short targets can become expensive or impossible to borrow.
Beta
A measure of how much a stock tends to move relative to a broad index — beta above 1 amplifies the index's moves, beta near 0 moves mostly independently.
IPO / SPAC / Lock-Up Period
An IPO is a company's first public share sale; a SPAC reaches the public market by merging into an existing shell company. A lock-up period (commonly 90–180 days) delays insiders from selling, and its expiration is a predictable volatility event.
ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund)
A basket of underlying assets — an index, sector, commodity, or strategy — that trades on an exchange with its own live price and chart, like a single stock.
Central Bank Rate Decision
A scheduled announcement (Fed, ECB, BOJ, etc.) setting the interest rate anchoring a currency's value — often the highest-impact scheduled event in forex.
Currency Correlation
The tendency of certain currency pairs (EUR/USD and GBP/USD, for example) to move together, meaning multiple "different" positions can secretly be the same underlying bet.
Prop Firm / Funded Account
A firm that allocates its own capital to traders who pass a rules-based evaluation, splitting profits in exchange for the trader following strict daily-loss and drawdown limits.
Slippage
The difference between a trade's expected price and its actual fill price, worst in fast-moving or thin markets where a market order "walks" through several order-book levels.
Pattern Day Trader (PDT)
A FINRA rule requiring at least $25,000 equity in a US margin account that executes 4+ day trades within 5 rolling business days — doesn't apply to futures accounts.
First Notice Date
The date after which a physically-settled commodity futures contract can trigger actual delivery obligations if not closed or rolled — irrelevant to cash-settled contracts like CME Bitcoin futures.
P/E Ratio
Price-to-earnings ratio — how much investors pay per dollar of a company's profit, a basic fundamental-analysis metric for comparing similar stocks.
Volume Profile / POC / Value Area
A histogram of volume by price rather than by time. The Point of Control (POC) is the single most-traded price; the Value Area is the range holding roughly 70% of total volume.
Portfolio Heat
The total percentage of an account at risk across every currently open position combined — not just any single trade's individual risk.
Personal Circuit Breaker
A pre-committed rule that halts all trading once an account hits a set drawdown threshold over a longer window (a week, a month) than a single bad day.
Initial Margin / Maintenance Margin
Initial margin is the deposit required to open a leveraged position; maintenance margin is the lower minimum equity required to keep it open — falling below it triggers a margin call.
Perpetual Futures ("Perp")
A futures contract with no expiration date, common on crypto exchanges — kept near the spot price by periodic funding payments instead of a convergence date.
Funding Rate
The periodic payment perpetual futures traders on one side (longs or shorts) make to the other, sized by how far the perp price has drifted from spot — a persistent cost or income tied to holding a perp, separate from price movement.
Fear & Greed Index
A blended 0–100 sentiment gauge (volatility, momentum, social activity, and more) used as a contrarian read — extreme greed often marks a crowded, late-stage rally; extreme fear often marks capitulation.
Price-Time Priority
The rule an exchange's matching engine uses to fill orders: best price first, and among orders at the same price, whoever placed theirs first gets filled first.
Dark Pool
A private trading venue that doesn't publicly display resting orders before execution, used mainly by large institutional traders to fill big orders with less market impact.
Order Routing
A broker's process of sending an order to whichever exchange or venue offers the best available price and liquidity at that moment, often splitting it across several.
Institutional Investor
A large pool of capital — a mutual fund, pension fund, or insurance company — trading in size, often executing big orders slowly to avoid moving price against itself.
Hedge Fund
An actively managed investment fund that can take both long and short positions with leverage, in contrast to more restricted institutional funds.
High-Frequency Trading (HFT)
Automated trading that holds positions for seconds or less, profiting from tiny, extremely fast price differences and providing much of the market's continuous liquidity.
Primary Market
Where new shares are created and sold for the first time (an IPO or follow-on offering) — the only point at which the issuing company itself receives the proceeds.
Secondary Market
Everywhere trading happens after issuance — investors buying and selling existing shares among themselves, which is the vast majority of all trading volume.
Follow-on Offering
A company selling additional new shares after its IPO, raising further cash directly but diluting existing shareholders.
Time & Sales ("The Tape")
A running list of every trade that has actually executed, with price, size, and time — distinct from the order book, which shows only orders that haven't traded yet.
Block Trade
A single unusually large trade print on the tape, often reflecting institutional-size activity rather than routine retail flow.
Market-Cap-Weighted Index
An index (like the S&P 500) that weights each member by total market value, so the largest companies dominate its moves.
Price-Weighted Index
An index (like the Dow Jones Industrial Average) that simply averages member share prices, so a high-priced stock moves it more regardless of company size.
Index Rebalancing
An index provider's periodic review adding growing companies and removing shrinking ones — an addition can cause real mechanical buying from funds tracking that index.
Ex-Dividend Date
The date on or after which buying a stock no longer entitles you to the next dividend payment; the stock typically opens lower that morning by roughly the dividend amount.
Stock Split / Reverse Split
A split multiplies share count and divides price proportionally (more, cheaper shares); a reverse split does the opposite, often to avoid falling below an exchange's minimum price. Neither changes the company's actual value.
Large-Cap / Mid-Cap / Small-Cap
Categories by total market value — large-cap companies are generally established and stable, small-caps more volatile with less analyst coverage.
Growth Stock / Value Stock
Growth stocks trade at higher valuations on expected future earnings growth and are more rate-sensitive; value stocks trade cheaply relative to current earnings or assets.
Blue Chip
A large, financially stable, long-track-record company, typically large-cap and value-oriented.
Penny Stock
A typically sub-$5 stock, often thinly traded and carrying elevated risk of volatility and manipulation.
Bond / Coupon / Yield
A bond is a loan to a government or company; the coupon is its periodic interest payment; yield is the effective return, which moves inversely to the bond's price.
Yield Curve / Inverted Yield Curve
A plot of bond yields across maturities, normally sloping upward; an inversion (short-term yields above long-term) has historically preceded most US recessions.
SEC / FINRA / CFTC / NFA
US regulators: the SEC oversees stock markets and disclosure; FINRA self-regulates brokerage firms; the CFTC oversees futures and derivatives (including CME Bitcoin futures); the NFA is the CFTC's self-regulatory counterpart to FINRA.
SIPC
Insurance protecting brokerage cash and securities (not market losses) up to a set limit if a US brokerage itself fails.
Trade Balance
The difference between a country's exports and imports; a sustained surplus tends to support its currency, a sustained deficit to weaken it.
Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)
The long-run theory that identical goods should cost the same across countries once exchange rates are accounted for — a slow-moving pull real prices constantly drift from.
Safe Haven Currency
A currency (USD, JPY, CHF) that tends to strengthen during broad risk-off periods regardless of that country's own data, as capital flees to perceived safety.
Line Chart / Bar Chart (OHLC)
A line chart connects only closing prices; a bar chart packs the same open/high/low/close data as a candlestick into tick marks instead of a colored body — same information, different visual style.
Heikin-Ashi
A candle style that averages each candle's open/close with the prior candle's values before plotting, smoothing trend noise — its values no longer match real traded prices, so it shouldn't be used to place an actual stop.
Renko Chart
A chart that ignores time entirely, plotting a new brick only once price moves a fixed amount — filters out small chop at the cost of losing any sense of how long a move took.
Delta
An option Greek measuring how much its price moves per $1 move in the underlying; also a rough approximation of the option's probability of expiring in the money.
Theta
An option Greek measuring time decay — the value lost per day as expiration approaches, accelerating in the final weeks before expiry.
Gamma
An option Greek measuring how fast Delta itself changes as the underlying moves, highest for at-the-money options near expiration.
Vega
An option Greek measuring sensitivity to implied volatility — a spike in expected volatility can move an option's price even before the underlying moves.
Private Key
The cryptographic secret that proves ownership of and authorizes spending from a crypto address — whoever holds it controls the coins, regardless of any account name attached.
Custodial Wallet
A wallet (typically on an exchange) where a third party holds the private key on the user's behalf — convenient, but exposed if that party is hacked or becomes insolvent.
Self-Custody (Hot / Cold Wallet)
Holding your own private key rather than a third party's — a hot wallet stays on an internet-connected device, a cold (hardware) wallet stays offline for greater security.
Stablecoin
A crypto token designed to hold a steady value, usually pegged 1:1 to the US dollar (e.g. USDT, USDC) and backed by issuer-held reserves.
Depeg
A stablecoin trading meaningfully away from its intended peg (e.g. away from $1) — a rare but real systemic risk signal about the issuer, not the broader market.
On-Chain Metrics
Indicators built directly from public blockchain data rather than price alone — Bitcoin's equivalent of the financial statements a company's stock is analyzed against.
MVRV
Market Value to Realized Value — compares Bitcoin's market cap to the aggregate price paid by current holders; historically stretches high near market tops and low near capitulation.
SOPR
Spent Output Profit Ratio — measures whether coins moving on-chain right now are, on average, being sold at a profit (above 1) or a loss (below 1).
Exchange Netflow
The net movement of coins onto exchanges (often preceding selling) versus off exchanges into self-custody (often read as accumulation).
Quantitative Easing (QE) / Tightening (QT)
QE is a central bank buying assets to inject money into the financial system, pushing yields down and supporting risk assets; QT reverses this, withdrawing liquidity.
Reserve Requirement
The portion of deposits a bank must hold rather than lend out — a less frequently used central-bank policy lever alongside the interest rate and QE/QT.
Commission
A flat or per-unit fee a broker charges to execute a trade, separate from the bid-ask spread or any slippage.
Swap / Rollover Fee
A cost (or credit) applied to a leveraged position held overnight, common in forex; the crypto-perpetual equivalent is the funding rate.
Overfitting (Curve-Fitting)
Tuning a backtested rule with so many specific parameters that it perfectly explains past data by memorizing its noise, rather than capturing a real, repeatable edge.
Survivorship Bias
A backtest flaw where only assets or companies that still exist today are included, silently excluding failures and inflating apparent historical performance.
Out-of-Sample Testing
Testing a rule on data it was never tuned on — the only honest check for whether a backtested edge is real rather than overfit.
Business Cycle
The recurring four-phase pattern of economic activity — expansion, peak, contraction, and trough — that rate decisions, the yield curve, and risk sentiment all reflect.
Asset Allocation
The decision of how to divide capital across broad categories (stocks, bonds, cash, crypto) before picking any individual position — the layer above single-trade risk management.
Rebalancing
Periodically selling what's grown and buying what's lagged to restore a portfolio's original target percentages — a mechanical way to capture "buy low, sell high."
Inflation / CPI
Inflation is the rate at which general prices rise, eroding currency's purchasing power; CPI is the index that measures it by pricing a fixed basket of goods over time.
Nominal vs Real Return
A nominal return is the raw percentage gain before adjustments; a real return subtracts inflation to show the actual change in purchasing power.
EPS (Earnings Per Share)
A company's net profit divided by its shares outstanding — the headline number compared against analyst consensus to judge an earnings "beat" or "miss."
Guidance
A company's own forward-looking estimate for the next quarter or year, given alongside earnings — often moves the stock more than the results that already happened.
Balance Sheet
A snapshot at one date of everything a company owns (assets), owes (liabilities), and has left for shareholders (equity), where Assets always equal Liabilities plus Equity.
Current Ratio / Debt-to-Equity Ratio
Current ratio (current assets ÷ current liabilities) checks short-term solvency; debt-to-equity (liabilities ÷ equity) measures how much of a company is funded by debt versus owner capital.
Bitcoin Dominance (BTC.D)
Bitcoin's market cap as a percentage of the total crypto market cap — rising when capital concentrates in Bitcoin, falling during "altcoin season" rotations.
Altcoin / Altcoin Season
Any cryptocurrency that isn't Bitcoin; "altcoin season" is a phase where altcoins collectively outperform Bitcoin as BTC.D falls.
Compound Interest
Interest earned on interest already earned, not just on the original amount — the reason growth accelerates the longer money stays invested.
Rule of 72
A mental-math shortcut: divide 72 by an annual percentage rate to estimate how many years it takes to double (or, applied to inflation, to halve).
Tax-Loss Harvesting
Deliberately selling a losing position to realize the loss for tax purposes, offsetting realized gains elsewhere and reducing the total tax owed.
Wash-Sale Rule
A US tax rule disallowing a claimed loss if a substantially identical security is bought back within 30 days before or after the sale.
